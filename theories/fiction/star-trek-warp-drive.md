# Star Trek: warp drive (1966 onward)

*A subspace field carries the ship at speeds numbered by warp factor on a scale that runs to infinity at warp 10; the drive that gave the physics its name.*

**Kind:** fiction · **Loophole:** L2 · **Notability:** N1 · **Coherence:** F3 · **Causality:** C-silent · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-12

## The drive as depicted

Star Trek never defines its drive in a single scene; it defines it by six decades of consistent usage, one reference book that the production treated as canon in most respects, and a handful of episodes that state rules. The first pilot, "The Cage" (1964, aired within "The Menagerie" in 1966), speaks of time warp factor and hyperdrive; by the series proper the terms are warp drive and warp factor, with warp 1 as the speed of light and higher factors as faster **[HIGH] S3** (Memory Alpha, sourced to the episodes). The ship is enclosed in a warp field generated by coils in its nacelles, fed by the plasma of a matter-antimatter reaction regulated by dilithium; the field is a distortion of subspace, a domain underlying normal space that Star Trek uses for both propulsion and communication, and the ship rides the distortion rather than accelerating through space **[MED] S3** for the on-screen usage, **[HIGH] S2** for the Technical Manual's account. *Star Trek: First Contact* (1996) shows the first flight: Zefram Cochrane's *Phoenix*, a converted Titan II missile, breaks the warp barrier on 5 April 2063 and its cockpit display reads space warp generator **[HIGH] S1**. An *Enterprise* hallucination in "Doctor's Orders" gives the plainest on-screen description, that the engine was designed to manipulate space and violate the laws of motion **[HIGH] S1**.

The scale is the drive's most articulated rule. The original series used a cubic scale, warp factor cubed times c, laid down in the writers' guide and reported in Whitfield's *The Making of Star Trek* (1968) **[MED] S2**. *Star Trek: The Next Generation Technical Manual* (Sternbach and Okuda, Pocket Books, 1991) recalibrated it: below warp 9 the speed goes as the warp factor to the power 10/3, above warp 9 the exponent rises steeply so that the curve is asymptotic to warp 10, and warp 10 is infinite speed, an energy barrier rather than a velocity barrier, so that ever higher speeds are written as 9.9, 9.99 and so on **[HIGH] S2** for the chart on page 55 and the asymptote, **[MED] S3** for the 10/3 exponent, which Okuda gave in correspondence reported by the rec.arts.startrek.tech FAQ. The manual records that Cochrane's original term was continuum distortion propulsion (page 54), that integer warp factors are more efficient than fractional ones because the field is built of nested layers each of which costs a step in power, and that the *Enterprise*-D's flight in "Where No One Has Gone Before" reached about warp 9.9999999996 rather than warp 10 (page 55) **[HIGH] S2** for the page citations as given by Memory Alpha, **[MED] S2** for the layered-field account, from memory of the manual and not re-read for this dossier. On screen, "Threshold" (*Voyager*, 1996) makes warp 10 the transwarp threshold at which a vessel occupies every point in the universe at once, and *Voyager*'s stated maximum, warp 9.975, is given in "Caretaker" (1995) **[HIGH] S1**. "The 37's" (*Voyager*, 1995) puts a number on warp 9.9, about 21,473 times c, in dialogue **[HIGH] S1**.

The one episode that gives warp a cost is "Force of Nature" (*The Next Generation* 7x09, first aired 15 November 1993, written by Naren Shankar): two Hekaran scientists show that warp fields damage the fabric of subspace, a rift opens in the Hekaras Corridor, and the Federation responds with a galaxy-wide limit of warp 5 except in emergencies **[HIGH] S1** for the episode, **[HIGH] S3** for the air date and credits. The limit was mentioned a few times afterwards and then quietly dropped when the *Voyager* and *Enterprise*-E designs were said to have solved the problem.

## Origin and lineage

Gene Roddenberry's series premiered on NBC on 8 September 1966; the word warp for a space-bending drive predates it, and the catalogue's [Campbell 1931](word-hyperspace-campbell-1931.md) entry records the earlier word hyperspace; Wikipedia's article on the Alcubierre drive credits Campbell's *Islands of Space* (1931) with the warp drive concept, citing Gardiner (2008) **[MED] S3**. Sternbach and Okuda's 1991 manual is the closest thing to an in-world definition and is the S2 source for this dossier.

The lineage that matters runs the other way. Miguel Alcubierre's 1994 letter says in its abstract that its propulsion mechanism is "reminiscent of the 'warp drive' of science fiction" **[HIGH] S1** (arXiv:gr-qc/0009013). That is the only primary statement located for this dossier. Popular accounts go further: David Darling's encyclopedia attributes to Alcubierre the words "I was watching Star Trek and thought there must be a way to do this right", without a source **[LOW] S3**; *Scientific American* (Gast, October 2021) calls him a passionate Star Trek devotee at the time of the paper **[MED] S3**; and Wikipedia reports an email from Alcubierre to William Shatner saying the theory was directly inspired by the show's term, citing Alan Shapiro's blog, which could not be opened for this dossier **[LOW] S5**. No interview transcript or recording in which Alcubierre himself names Star Trek was found as of 2026-09-12; the abstract is sufficient to establish that the name was borrowed from fiction, and the register's [WRP-1](../../constraints/wrp-1.md) and the [Alcubierre 1994](../physics/alcubierre-1994.md) dossier say the same. This dossier and that one are the two ends of the lineage page planned as star-trek-alcubierre-elite-orville; the downstream fictions that adopted the physics by name are [Elite Dangerous](elite-dangerous-frame-shift-drive.md) and [The Orville](the-orville-quantum-drive.md).

## Which loophole, and why

L2, move the space. The ship does not accelerate through space; a field distorts subspace around it and the distortion carries the ship, which is why warp travel involves no time dilation, why the crew feel nothing and why the *Phoenix*'s cockpit calls it a space warp generator. Subspace itself is an L4 flavour, a domain beneath normal space, and Star Trek's subspace radio ([Star Trek subspace radio](star-trek-subspace-radio.md)) is its L5 use; but the ships stay in normal space inside their fields, so L2 is primary. The physics cousin is [Alcubierre 1994](../physics/alcubierre-1994.md), and the bill is the register's: negative energy in the bubble wall, which under the quantum inequalities is about $-6 \times 10^{62}$ kg for a hundred-metre bubble ([ENE-3](../../constraints/ene-3.md)); a horizon at the wall for any superluminal speed, so the crew cannot steer ([CON-1](../../constraints/con-1.md)); a route that must be laid in advance ([CON-2](../../constraints/con-2.md)); and two bubbles making a time machine ([CAU-3](../../constraints/cau-3.md)). Star Trek's nacelle coils and dilithium are the fiction's stand-in for the exotic matter, and the manual's nested field layers are its stand-in for the shaping function. [Van Den Broeck 1999](../physics/van-den-broeck-1999.md) cuts the bill to a few solar masses; nothing in the register gets it lower for a superluminal bubble.

## The rules it keeps

- **The warp scale.** Warp factors map to speeds, higher factors cost more, and the mapping was published, applied for decades and revised once (the 1991 asymptote at warp 10) rather than abandoned **[HIGH] S2**. Individual episodes contradict the chart routinely; the rule survives the exceptions.
- **Warp 10 is a barrier.** "Threshold" states it and pays for crossing it with hyper-evolution, and the show never crosses it again **[HIGH] S1**.
- **Warp has an environmental cost.** "Force of Nature" invents subspace damage and a speed limit, applied in later dialogue and then engineered away **[HIGH] S1**.
- **The field needs a source.** Matter-antimatter reaction, dilithium regulation, nacelle coils: warp cores fail, breach and are ejected, and a ship without one is stranded **[HIGH] S1**.
- **No dilation.** Crews at warp share the calendar of the planets they leave and return to, throughout the franchise **[HIGH] S1**.

That is F3: limitations stated, numbered and applied with reasonable consistency, with physical vocabulary but no engagement with a real constraint. The manual's account of the field is mechanism-shaped without being a mechanism, and no episode names negative energy, horizons or the control problem.

## What it ignores

Frames. In six decades no Star Trek instalment has considered that a ship arriving faster than light arrives before it left for some observer, and the franchise's single Starfleet clock (stardates) is assumed, never justified. Star Trek does use warp for time travel, which is why the mark needs a sentence: the slingshot effect, in "Tomorrow Is Yesterday" (1967), "Assignment: Earth" (1968) and *Star Trek IV* (1986), sends a ship through time by running at high warp toward a star and breaking away, and McCoy states the rule in the film, that enough speed round the sun puts you in time warp **[HIGH] S1**. That is a warp drive plus a gravity well making a time machine, which is the shape of [CAU-3](../../constraints/cau-3.md), and it is scored there as engagement. It is not ownership of CAU-1: the show never connects the effect to the relativity of simultaneity, never says which frame the slingshot runs against and never lets ordinary warp travel disturb the order of events. C-silent, dated 2026-09-12 across the original series, the films, TNG, DS9, Voyager, Enterprise, Discovery, Picard and Strange New Worlds.
