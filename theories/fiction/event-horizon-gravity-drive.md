# Event Horizon (1997): the gravity drive

*A ship that makes its own black hole to fold space between two points, explained with a folded sheet of paper, and which comes back from somewhere worse than nowhere.*

**Kind:** fiction · **Loophole:** L3 · **Notability:** N2 · **Coherence:** F1 · **Causality:** C-silent · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-12

## The drive as depicted

*Event Horizon* is a 1997 film directed by Paul W. S. Anderson from a screenplay by Philip Eisner, set in 2047. The title ship vanished seven years earlier on her maiden voyage to Proxima Centauri and has reappeared in orbit around Neptune; the rescue ship *Lewis and Clark* is sent to find out why, with the drive's designer, Dr William Weir, aboard **[HIGH] S1**. The drive is defined in one scene on the way out, when Weir briefs the rescue crew. Starck objects that relativity forbids faster-than-light travel. Weir answers that the ship does not break the law, it goes around it: the ship never moves faster than light, it creates a "dimensional gateway" through which it jumps from one point in the universe to another, light years away **[HIGH] S1**. Asked how, he gives the technical version, a rotating magnetic field focusing a narrow beam of gravitons that fold spacetime until the curvature becomes infinite and a singularity forms, and then the version everyone remembers. He marks two points on a sheet of paper, asks for the shortest distance between them, rejects "a straight line" and folds the paper so the points touch, pushing a pen through both. "The shortest distance between two points is zero" (Weir, briefing scene) **[HIGH] S1**. The singularity, he says, folds space so that the two points coexist, and once the ship passes through the gateway space returns to normal. He names it: "It's called a gravity drive" **[HIGH] S1**.

The drive itself is shown as a spherical core, a set of nested rotating rings around a containment sphere, at the heart of the ship behind a corridor of rotating blades **[HIGH] S1**. The film gives no numbers: no energy, no mass, no transit time, no range beyond "light years". What it does give is the outcome. The ship's log shows the crew engaging the drive to "open the gateway" on the way to Proxima Centauri, and then the crew killing each other; the recovered signal reads "libera te tutemet ex inferis", save yourself from hell **[HIGH] S1**. The film's position, stated by the crew's doctor and then by Weir, is that the gateway did not lead to Proxima Centauri but to somewhere outside the known universe, and that the ship brought something back with it **[HIGH] S1**. In the last act the drive activates on its own, opens the gateway as a visible black hole that pulls in the stern of the ship, and the film ends with the survivors adrift **[HIGH] S1**.

Two things are notable about the depiction. First, the drive's entire physical rationale is delivered in one speech and never revisited; nobody asks what the singularity is made of, how the gateway is aimed or why the ship survives its own black hole. Second, the film is explicit that the drive is a shortcut, not a fast engine: Weir's own words rule out local superluminal motion. The genre took the folded-paper demonstration and kept it. The same demonstration reappears in *Interstellar* (2014) and *Thor: Love and Thunder* (2022) **[MED] S3**.

## Origin and lineage

Eisner drafted the script in 1992 as a haunted house in space; Anderson reworked it after *Mortal Kombat*, pulling it away from *Alien* and towards *The Haunting*, *The Shining* and *Hellraiser*, and it was shot at Pinewood between November 1996 and March 1997 **[HIGH] S3** (Wikipedia, citing the production interviews). The drive was never the point of the film and there is no record of a science consultant. The technical line about gravitons and "Weyl tensor dynamics" is in the shooting script as Eisner wrote it **[HIGH] S1**. The Weyl tensor is real, the part of spacetime curvature that is not fixed by local matter, and the name is used here as decoration; nothing in the film depends on it.

The folded-paper demonstration is older than the film. Madeleine L'Engle's *A Wrinkle in Time* (1962) has Mrs Whatsit explain the tesseract by folding the hem of a skirt so an ant can cross without walking the length **[MED] S3**. Whether Eisner had it in mind is not sourced, and this dossier does not claim it. What is sourced is the forward lineage: the film's version became the standard on-screen shorthand for a wormhole, reused by Nolan's science-consulted *Interstellar* **[MED] S3**. No real proposal is named in the film. The only physics vocabulary is "singularity", "gravitons" and the Weyl tensor.

The gravity drive gyroscope was modelled on the puzzle box from *Hellraiser* **[MED] S3**, which says more about what the drive is for than any equation would.

## Which loophole, and why

**L3, take a shortcut.** Weir says so in terms: the ship does not move faster than light, it makes two distant points coincide and steps across. The mechanism is a singularity produced on board, which puts the film in the black-hole-as-tunnel family rather than the traversable-wormhole family. Its nearest physics cousin is therefore [passage through the Kerr interior](../physics/kerr-interior-passage.md), the one general-relativity construction in which a black hole is a door to somewhere else. That dossier's bill is B-mass and B-caus: a rotating hole of tens of thousands of solar masses at minimum, a route that runs through a region of built-in closed timelike curves, and a destination nobody chooses. The film keeps the last part faithfully.

The honest wormhole cousin is [Morris and Thorne 1988](../physics/morris-thorne-1988.md), whose bill is negative energy at the throat in neutron-star-core quantities, a far mouth carried to the destination at sublight speed before the first trip and conversion to a time machine by any round trip of a mouth. The film's drive pays none of these on screen: the gateway is created from one end, on demand, with no far mouth. A gate opened from one side only is exactly the object [CON-2](../../constraints/con-2.md) says cannot be aimed.

There is a secondary move. The gateway is called "dimensional" and the place it leads is outside the universe, which is L4 language. The film uses it as horror, not as transport, and the drive as a transport device is scored as L3.

## The rules it keeps

- The ship never moves faster than light locally. Weir states it, and nothing in the film contradicts it **[HIGH] S1**.
- The drive is a shortcut with a beginning and an end: the ship jumps, the gateway closes, space returns to normal **[HIGH] S1**.
- The gateway needs the drive to be engaged, and engaging it is a decision taken by the crew, with a countdown **[HIGH] S1**.
- The destination is not where you aimed. Set for Proxima Centauri, the ship went somewhere else, and the film never lets anyone say where **[HIGH] S1**.

That is the whole rulebook, and the last rule undoes the first three as engineering. The drive works when the horror needs it to, including switching itself on in the finale. The paper demonstration is a good explainer of what a wormhole is; it is not a limitation the story then respects, because the story's interest is in what came back, not in what it cost to go. Hence F1: the drive is a handwave with a memorable diagram. It is not F0, because the film takes its drive entirely seriously.

## What it ignores

The film never raises time. A ship that jumps light years and returns has, by the register's first entry, sent a message into somebody's past, and a ship that has been missing for seven years by Earth clocks is an invitation to ask how long the trip took by hers. The log shows the crew dying soon after the jump and the film leaves the seven-year gap unexplained; no character asks whether the ship's clocks agree with Neptune's. The seven years are a horror beat, not a relativistic one **[HIGH] S1**. The mark is C-silent.

The register would also ask what the singularity is made of, what holds the gateway open, why the ship at the centre of its own black hole is not torn apart and how a gateway aimed at Proxima Centauri was aimed at all. None is addressed. The sole physical engagement the film offers is Starck's objection from relativity and Weir's answer, which is the L3 move stated cleanly and then abandoned.
