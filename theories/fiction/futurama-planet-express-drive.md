# Futurama: the Planet Express ship's dark matter engines (1999 onward)

*The engines don't move the ship; the ship stays where it is and the engines move the universe around it, which is a warp drive described exactly and as a joke.*

**Kind:** fiction · **Loophole:** L2 · **Notability:** N2 · **Coherence:** F0 · **Causality:** C-silent · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-12

## The drive as depicted

The Planet Express ship is defined in one scene of "A Clone of My Own" (production code 2ACV10, first aired 9 April 2000) **[HIGH] S1** for the dialogue, **[MED] S3** for the code and date. Standing on the hull, Professor Farnsworth shows his clone Cubert the dark matter engines he invented, which let his ship travel between galaxies in hours. Cubert objects that nothing can go faster than the speed of light, and Farnsworth agrees: that is why scientists increased the speed of light in 2208 **[HIGH] S1**. Cubert says that is impossible too. At the end of the episode, having learned that nothing is impossible if you can imagine it, Cubert announces that he understands the engines: "The ship stays where it is and the engines move the universe around it" **[HIGH] S1**. Bender calls this a complete load; the ship then takes off in a slow-motion sequence in which the universe visibly moves past a stationary ship.

The fuel is dark matter, established in "Love's Labours Lost in Space" (1ACV04, 1999): a super-dense substance mined out of the planet Vergon 6, each pound of which weighs over ten thousand pounds, valuable as starship fuel, and, as the crew discover, excreted by Nibbler **[HIGH] S1**. The film *Bender's Game* (2008) later renders all dark matter inert when the Professor's crystals are destroyed **[MED] S3**. The engines otherwise do what the plot requires: the ship crosses the galaxy in an episode, reaches other galaxies when a story needs one and runs out of fuel when a story needs that.

## Origin and lineage

Futurama was created by Matt Groening and David X. Cohen for Fox and premiered on 28 March 1999; "A Clone of My Own" was written by Patric M. Verrone **[MED] S3**. The writers' room famously included several people with mathematics and physics degrees and the show's science jokes are usually jokes about real ideas. Cubert's line is one of them. It describes, almost verbatim, the [Alcubierre 1994](../physics/alcubierre-1994.md) metric, in which the ship sits at rest on a timelike geodesic inside a flat region and it is the geometry that carries the region; Alcubierre's own gloss is that the ship never moves faster than light locally and that the distortion does the travelling. Whether the writers had the paper in mind is not documented; the episode aired six years after it, the phrase "warp drive" was in the news, and the joke works either way. No influence is asserted. Farnsworth's line about raising the speed of light is a separate joke about the same fact the [Scharnhorst effect](../physics/scharnhorst-effect.md) illustrates, that c is a property of the vacuum and could in principle be different; the increase in 2208 is never mentioned again in the series **[HIGH] S1**.

## Which loophole, and why

L2, move the space, on the strength of Cubert's line, which is the show's only statement of mechanism and is the L2 move in plain words. Farnsworth's line points at L1: if c were raised, an ordinary ship could go faster than old light without breaking anything. The two jokes are not reconciled and the dossier follows the catalogue in taking the more famous one as primary. The physics cousin is [Alcubierre 1994](../physics/alcubierre-1994.md) and the bill is the register's: negative energy in the wall of order $10^{62}$ kg for a ship-sized bubble ([ENE-3](../../constraints/ene-3.md)), a horizon that makes the drive uncontrollable from the pilot's seat ([CON-1](../../constraints/con-1.md)), a route laid in advance ([CON-2](../../constraints/con-2.md)) and a time machine from two bubbles ([CAU-3](../../constraints/cau-3.md)). Dark matter, in the show, is at least heavy in the right spirit; the register's bill is negative, not dense. For the L1 reading the cousin is the [Scharnhorst effect](../physics/scharnhorst-effect.md), whose bill is that the raise is one part in $10^{32}$ and that the plates pick a frame ([CAU-4](../../constraints/cau-4.md)).

## The rules it keeps

Two, both jokes and both kept. The ship needs dark matter, which is scarce, heavy, excreted by a small animal and later abolished; fuel is a recurring plot device from the fourth episode to the third film **[HIGH] S1**. And the ship does not move: the take-off sequence in "A Clone of My Own" animates the universe sliding past a stationary ship, and the show never contradicts Cubert **[HIGH] S1**. Everything else, speed, range, travel time, is whatever the episode needs. That is a perfect F0: the drive is the punchline, the show knows it and the punchline happens to be true to the physics it is mocking.

## What it ignores

The frame question, and everything adjacent to it. Futurama has time travel in "Roswell That Ends Well" (2001), by a supernova and a microwave, and throughout *Bender's Big Score* (2007), by a time code, and it plays paradoxes for laughs; none of it involves the engines, and no episode connects faster-than-light flight to the order of events **[HIGH] S1**. The single clock of the year 3000 is assumed. C-silent, dated 2026-09-12 across the original run, the films and the revivals.
