# Space Battleship Yamato: warp by wave motion engine

*The 1974 anime's rebuilt battleship crosses 148,000 light-years and back inside a year on an engine built from alien plans; the same engine fires the ship's great gun, and every warp is a leap the crew feel.*

**Kind:** fiction · **Loophole:** L4 · **Notability:** N3 · **Coherence:** F2 · **Causality:** C-silent · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-12

## The drive as depicted

*Space Battleship Yamato* (*Uchū Senkan Yamato*) ran on Yomiuri Television from 6 October 1974 to 30 March 1975, twenty-six episodes cut down from a planned thirty-nine, produced by Yoshinobu Nishizaki with Leiji Matsumoto directing and designing **[HIGH] S3**. The premise sets the drive's terms. Earth has been bombed radioactive by Gamilas; a capsule from Queen Starsha of Iscandar, 148,000 light-years away, offers the Cosmo Cleaner (Cosmo DNA in the English dub) that can undo it, and contains the plans for an engine that can "propel a ship at faster-than-light speeds" **[HIGH] S3**. Humanity has one year before the underground cities fail, and the series counts the days down at the end of every episode **[HIGH] S3**. The engine, the wave motion engine (*hadō enjin*), is built into the raised wreck of the battleship Yamato, and the series is the round trip.

The first warp is episode 4, "World of Wonder! Yamato Leaps Past Light!", broadcast 27 October 1974 **[HIGH] S3**. The ship, having reached the Moon under ordinary thrust, must time the warp precisely, and the bridge crew count it in; the episode shows the warp as a passage between the third dimension and the fourth, with the ship stretching and the stars streaming, during which the crew see visions and the ship's clocks and the crew's sense of time slip **[MED] S3**. The ship emerges where the calculation said it would, the bridge confirms the jump against the charts, and the next episode opens at Jupiter **[MED] S3**. Later warps carry the ship further per jump, and the dialogue treats warp distance as a calculation, with the navigator plotting each leap against the ship's charts and the engine's readiness **[MED] S3**. The same engine feeds the ship's principal weapon, the wave motion gun, which fires the engine's accumulated energy from the bow; firing it leaves the ship without power for propulsion or warp for a period, which episode 5's escape from the floating continent of Jupiter dramatises **[MED] S3**.

The reference literature attributes the engine's energy to tachyon particles, drawn from space and converted into "wave motion energy"; this catalogue has not verified the word against the 1974 scripts and records it as a fan-and-reference gloss **[LOW] S3**. The 2012 remake *Space Battleship Yamato 2199* restates the drive with more vocabulary, gives the Gamilas their own "Geschtam jump" and makes the wave motion engine's use as a weapon a moral question; the 1974 series is the one scored here, and the remake is noted only for what it adds **[MED] S3**. The American adaptation *Star Blazers* (1979) carried the drive, renamed nothing about it and taught a generation of English-speaking viewers the word "warp" alongside *Star Trek* **[HIGH] S3**.

## Origin and lineage

Nishizaki began developing the project in 1973 and the series premiered against *Heidi* and lost the ratings battle, which is why the run was cut; it became a phenomenon in reruns and through the 1977 compilation film, and is generally credited with starting the anime boom of the late 1970s **[HIGH] S3**. The word "warp" (*wāpu*) was already loose in Japanese science fiction from *Star Trek*'s Japanese broadcast (1969), and the series uses it for a jump rather than for a continuous speed, which is the L4 reading rather than *Star Trek*'s L2 one; no statement by the production tying the drive to a real proposal has been found and none is claimed. The influence out of the series is broad but unsourced beyond the general histories: the space-opera anime that followed (*Galaxy Express 999*, *Macross*, *Gundam*) took "warp" as a given.

Lineage: [Feinberg's tachyon, outward](../../lineage/feinberg-tachyon-outward.md).

## Which loophole, and why

L4, leave the manifold, on the series' own picture: the ship leaves ordinary space, passes through a region in which the crew's senses and clocks misbehave, and reappears at a calculated point, with the passage described in the dimensional language of "third to fourth". Nothing accelerates the ship past c in normal space (L1); the engine does not carry a bubble of space with it (L2), and the visual, the ship streaming into a tunnel of light and out again, is the L4 convention. The "fold" reading, in which the jump is a shortcut through folded space (L3), belongs to *Macross* (1982) and is not what the 1974 series says.

The physics cousins are the extra-dimension dossiers. [The Kaluza-Klein hyperspace reading](../physics/kaluza-klein-hyperspace.md) is the negative result: the fourth spatial dimension of the series' own explanation, if it existed as physics knows extra dimensions, would be compact or warped and would share the ship's light cone, so there is no leap through it; the bill of the class is B-new. [Brane-bulk shortcuts](../physics/brane-bulk-shortcuts.md) is the one real thing of the kind, a bulk route that beats the brane by one part in $10^{58}$, at a bill of B-new, B-mass and B-caus. If the tachyon gloss is right the third cousin is [Feinberg's tachyons](../physics/tachyons-feinberg-1967.md), whose finding is that the tachyon field is an instability rather than a courier and that any tachyonic particle would be an antitelephone; its standing is K0. The register entry for the class is [MAN-1](../../constraints/man-1.md).

## The rules it keeps

The Coherence mark is F2, rule-bound. The drive has consistent rules without physical content, and the series holds to them. A warp must be calculated and timed; the ship must be clear of a planet's neighbourhood; the engine must be charged, and charging takes time the plot counts; firing the wave motion gun uses the same energy and leaves the ship unable to warp until it recovers; each warp covers a finite, calculated distance, so the voyage is a chain of jumps with sublight legs between them, and the one-year clock makes every jump a budget decision **[MED] S3**. The countdown is the rule that makes the rest bite: the series knows exactly how far Iscandar is and how many days remain, and it shows the arithmetic. That a whole series of 1974 held a distance, a speed and a deadline consistent for twenty-six episodes is the reason it is filed here.

The rules are not physics. The series never says why a warp has a maximum distance, what the engine converts or what the fourth dimension is; the "warp" is a leap with a light show, and the crew's visions during it are atmosphere rather than a mechanism. The tachyon gloss, if it is in the scripts, would be the one nod to a real concept and it is used as a fuel name.

## What it ignores

The register's questions. No episode says what the wave motion engine does to spacetime, what the warp costs beyond charging time, how a jump is aimed or why the ship cannot simply warp the whole distance. The hazards shown during warp are visions and disorientation, never anything the register would recognise.

Causality is C-silent. The ship leaps 148,000 light-years and back within a year of Earth's calendar, the series' entire dramatic frame is a single shared countdown between the ship and Earth, and no episode asks whether "one year" means the same thing on both ends of a faster-than-light voyage, let alone what the antitelephone would do to the countdown. There is no stated preferred frame; the shared clock is assumed. Last checked 2026-09-12 across the 1974 series: the causal cost of warp is never raised, and the 2012 remake does not raise it either.
