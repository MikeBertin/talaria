# Feinberg's tachyon, outward

*Feinberg names the faster-than-light particle in 1967, having been set thinking by a 1954 story; the word then goes back into fiction, carrying its physics exactly once (Timescape) and travelling everywhere else as a noise that means "faster than light".*

**Kind:** lineage · **Span:** 1954–1991 · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-13

## The line

The line starts in fiction, which is the surprise. Gerald Feinberg told Gregory Benford that he had begun thinking about tachyons because he was "inspired by James Blish's short story 'Beep'", the 1954 story of a faster-than-light communicator whose signals go into the past, and that he "had set out to see if such a gadget was theoretically possible" **[HIGH] S2** (Benford, "Old Legends", 1995, p. 276, as quoted in Milburn's 2019 essay). The result was [Feinberg 1967](../theories/physics/tachyons-feinberg-1967.md), Phys. Rev. 159, 1089, which at p. 1090 presents a description "for noninteracting faster than light particles, which we call tachyons" **[HIGH] S2** (paper quoted by Milburn; the paper itself is paywalled), and which Feinberg glossed three years later: "I named them tachyons, from the Greek word tachys, meaning swift" **[HIGH] S2** (*Scientific American*, February 1970, p. 70, as quoted by Milburn). The physics behind the name is a Lorentz-invariant field with imaginary mass, no rest frame, energy that falls as speed rises and a vacuum that observers disagree about; the catalogue grades it K0, because the field turned out to describe an instability rather than a particle ([LOR-2](../constraints/lor-2.md)).

The word went back out almost at once, and it went out without the physics. Bob Shaw's *The Palace of Eternity* (1969) has "a million-ton tachyonic spaceship" at thirty thousand times light speed **[MED] S3** (Wikipedia, "Tachyons in fiction"), which is a use of the name for a starship drive that Feinberg's paper, a theory of particles that cannot slow down and cannot be ridden, does not license. Robert Silverberg's *Tower of Glass* (1970) has "tachyon-beam ultrawave communications equipment", and Joe Haldeman in 1973 has a "tachyon-torch shuttle" **[HIGH] S2** (Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction, entries "ultrawave" and "torch"). The one work that took the physics with the word is [Timescape](../theories/fiction/timescape-tachyon-message.md) (1980), written by Feinberg's fellow physicist Benford on the back of his own antitelephone paper; that descent has its own page, [tolman-benford-timescape](tolman-benford-timescape.md), and this page only notes that Benford set part of the novel at Columbia "because of Feinberg" **[HIGH] S1** (Timescape dossier, from "Old Legends").

Everywhere after that the word is a label. In *Watchmen* (1986 to 1987) Ozymandias floods his Antarctic base with tachyons so that Doctor Manhattan cannot see the future, "presumably because tachyons scramble cause and effect" **[MED] S3** (Wikipedia, "Tachyons in fiction"; issue 11 is cover-dated August 1987). Star Trek first says the word on screen in *The Next Generation*'s "Deja Q" (5 February 1990): "We're being hit by a field of energetic tachyons" **[HIGH] S1** (shooting script 40273-161, Worf's line; a scan of every earlier TNG transcript, every original-series transcript and the first five films found no earlier use). From "Redemption II" (1991) on, the tachyon detection grid, tachyon eddies and tachyon pulses are the franchise's spare part for anything faster than light, and the [subspace radio](../theories/fiction/star-trek-subspace-radio.md) and [warp drive](../theories/fiction/star-trek-warp-drive.md) dossiers record that the shows keep the name and drop the theory: nothing in the scripts has imaginary mass, no rest frame or a message to the past unless the plot wants one. *Space Battleship Yamato* (1974) is often said to run its wave motion engine on tachyons, but that gloss comes from later companion writing and fan wikis, not from anything the catalogue has traced to the 1974 broadcast ([Yamato](../theories/fiction/yamato-wave-motion-warp.md)) **[LOW] S3**. The plain finding of this line is that in most fiction the word travelled and the physics stayed home.

## What is not sourced

- **The paper's own words.** Feinberg's coinage is here at second hand, through Milburn's quotation of p. 1090 and of the 1970 *Scientific American* piece. Milburn is a peer-reviewed historian citing page numbers, which earns S2, but the paper is paywalled and was not opened; the catalogue's tachyon dossier has the same gap. A library copy of Phys. Rev. 159 would close it.
- **A dictionary entry for "tachyon".** The brief asked for the Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction's entry. A scan of the site's forty-nine index pages on 2026-09-13 found no headword "tachyon"; the word appears only inside citations for other entries. Either the entry does not exist yet or the index missed it. The SF Encyclopedia's entry "Tachyons" says only that the word "became an item of sf Terminology in the 1970s".
- **Shaw 1969 as first.** Wikipedia's survey gives *The Palace of Eternity* as the earliest fiction use; no dictionary citation backs it and the novel was not opened. An earlier 1968 magazine use would not be surprising.
- **Watchmen at issue level.** The Watchmen wiki and DC database could not be opened (paywalled fetch). The issue number and cover date come from search summaries of those pages and are marked accordingly.
- **Yamato and 1974.** Whether the word "tachyon" (*takion*) was spoken in the 1974 series or printed in its settings material, or whether later writers added it, is not established. The CosmoDNA page that carries the tachyon explanation is a translation of Yanagita's popular-science writing of unstated date. A 1974 script or the *Roman Album* would settle it.
- **Star Trek before 1990.** The transcript scan covered TNG seasons one to three up to "Deja Q", all seventy-nine original-series episodes and films one to five. The animated series and tie-in novels were not checked, so "first on screen" is stated for live action only.
