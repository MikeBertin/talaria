# Hyperspace: the word

*A mathematician's word for space with more than three dimensions (recorded from 1852) is borrowed by a pulp writer in 1928, made into a drive by Campbell in 1931, passed down the magazine he came to edit and made universal by Star Wars in 1977, while the physics it sounds like never meant any of it.*

**Kind:** lineage · **Span:** 1852–1977 · **Last reviewed:** 2026-09-13

## The line

The word came first and the idea came later. "Hyperspace" was a term of nineteenth-century geometry for a space of more than three dimensions, and the Oxford English Dictionary's third edition dates that sense to 1852 **[MED] S2** (as reported in the etymology note of the Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction; the OED itself is behind a paywall and was not opened). It is the same sense the physics of extra dimensions still uses, which is why the physics end of this line is [Kaluza-Klein and the hyperspace reading](../theories/physics/kaluza-klein-hyperspace.md): Kaluza's fifth dimension (1921) and Klein's compact circle (1926) are hyperspace in the 1852 meaning, more directions on the same manifold, and nothing else. The fiction sense, a separate region with its own rules that a ship can enter and leave, has no counterpart there, which is what the register's [MAN-1](../constraints/man-1.md) says.

The fiction sense grew in two steps. Kirk Meadowcroft's "The Invisible Bubble" (*Amazing Stories*, September 1928) used "hyper-space" as a place next door to ours, with "boundary lines" that "may not be so rigidly drawn as we have supposed", but nobody travels by it **[HIGH] S2** (Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction, entry "hyperspace", first two citations). Three years later John W. Campbell's *Islands of Space* (*Amazing Stories Quarterly*, Spring 1931) attached the word to a way of beating light, and that is the first such use the dictionary can find **[HIGH] S2**. The catalogue's dossier on that text, [Campbell 1931](../theories/fiction/word-hyperspace-campbell-1931.md), records the twist: Campbell's ship does not go anywhere. Its main coil affects "all the space about it" so that the crew are "enclosed in what might be called a hyperspace of our own making", and the ship then moves at any speed it pleases **[HIGH] S1** (chapter 5 of the revised text, Project Gutenberg 20988). In the catalogue's own terms that is an L2 move, a pocket of altered space carried with the ship, sixty-three years before Alcubierre, and not the L4 move the word came to mean. The one sentence that is certainly from 1931, "in the hyperspace we are going into, a new condition exists", is the dictionary's citation; the rest of the drive is read from the 1957 revision.

From Campbell the word spread through the magazines. Jack Williamson (*Wonder Stories*, May 1934) and Murray Leinster (*Astounding*, June 1934) used it within three years; then Campbell took over *Astounding* in 1937 and the next citations are all from his magazine: E. E. Smith's *Gray Lensman* (December 1939), Theodore Sturgeon's "Artnan Process" (June 1941), A. E. van Vogt's "Secret Unattainable" (July 1942) and, in book form, Isaac Asimov's *Foundation* (1951), whose stories Campbell had bought for *Astounding* from 1942 **[HIGH] S2** (dictionary citations; Campbell's editorship from Wikipedia). By Sturgeon the word means what it means now: a ship "slipped into hyperspace and traveled toward Procyon" at many times light speed, a place you enter and leave. [Asimov's Jump](../theories/fiction/foundation-hyperspace-jumps.md) fixed the pulp default, an instantaneous, computed crossing of a region "neither space nor time". The last step is the largest and the least documented. George Lucas's 1977 shooting script has Han Solo promise "we'll be safe enough once we make the jump to hyperspace" and the stage direction "stars become streaks as the pirateship makes the jump to hyperspace" **[HIGH] S1** (script as hosted at IMSDb); [Star Wars](../theories/fiction/star-wars-hyperspace.md) took a forty-six-year-old genre word and made it the one everybody knows. No document in which Lucas names the pulp source of the word has been found.

## What is not sourced

- **Who read whom.** The dictionary gives dates and venues, not readers. That Campbell had read Meadowcroft, that Williamson or Leinster had read Campbell, is inferred from shared magazines and three-year gaps. A letter, an interview or a memoir naming the source would settle each link; none was found.
- **The OED entry itself.** The 1852 date for the mathematical sense and the 1947 date for the OED's earliest fiction citation are both taken from the Historical Dictionary's note about the OED, not from the OED. Opening the OED's "hyperspace, n." would confirm both and give the 1852 text.
- **Campbell's 1931 wording.** The chapter 5 passage quoted here is from the 1957 Fantasy Press revision, which is the text Project Gutenberg carries. The 1931 magazine text has not been seen, so whether "a hyperspace of our own making" is 1931 or 1957 is open. The *Amazing Stories Quarterly* scan would settle it.
- **Lucas's source.** No interview or note in which Lucas says where he took "hyperspace" from. Wikipedia's survey of the film's sources names *Foundation* (with a citation needed) and the Lensman books (via a 2015 *Guardian* piece) as influences in general; neither mention is about the word. The link from pulp to film is genre inheritance, [LOW] until someone finds Lucas saying it.
- **Gardiner's 2008 JBIS paper**, credited by Wikipedia with saying that *Islands of Space* introduced hyperspace and the warp drive, was not opened.
