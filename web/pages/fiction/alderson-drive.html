The Alderson drive (Niven and Pournelle 1974)

Tramlines between stars along lines of equal thermonuclear flux, an instantaneous jump from one Alderson point to another, and a crew too dazed afterwards to fight.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L3 · Notability: N2 · Coherence: F3 · Causality: C-silent · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

The Mote in God's Eye (Larry Niven and Jerry Pournelle, Simon and Schuster, 1974) is the defining instalment, though the drive belongs to Pournelle's wider CoDominium setting [HIGH] S3. The novel states the rule in chapter 4, "Priority OC": the Alderson drive's pathways "are generated along lines of equipotential thermonuclear flux", which is to say that between two stars whose fusion output defines a line of equal potential there is a tramline, and a ship at the right point on it (the Alderson point) can switch on the drive and appear, with no elapsed time, at the corresponding point in the other system [MED] S1 (read in an online copy of the text). The prologue names the same tramline effect as what colonised the stars and made interstellar war possible [MED] S1. Not every pair of stars has a tramline, not every tramline is usable and the points lie well outside the habitable zone, so a journey between distant systems is a chain of jumps with sublight legs between them [HIGH] S3. The Alderson points are fixed by the stars; a ship must reach the point by ordinary flight and must be at it, not near it, to jump [HIGH] S3.

The jump has two costs the novel keeps throughout. The first is what the text calls "the characteristic confusion that always follows instantaneous travel": crew are disoriented for some minutes after a jump and ship's computers for longer, so a ship arriving at an Alderson point is helpless until it recovers, which is the tactical fact the novel's naval scenes are built on [MED] S1. The second is the Langston Field, a spherical field around a warship that absorbs incoming energy and re-radiates it, needed for combat and, in the plot, for survival: the only tramline out of the Mote system has its far Alderson point inside the outer layers of the red supergiant Murcheson's Eye, so any ship using it emerges inside a star [HIGH] S3. Human warships can survive there briefly on their Fields; the Moties, who have the drive but not the Field, have lost every ship they ever sent and call the drive the Crazy Eddie drive because ships vanish and never return [HIGH] S3. The asymmetry decides the book.

Origin and lineage

Pournelle named the drive for Dan Alderson, a Jet Propulsion Laboratory scientist and friend, who worked out its notional rules to the authors' specification: Niven and Pournelle listed what they wanted the drive to allow and to forbid, and Alderson designed a scheme with those properties and some added limits of its own [MED] S3 (the account is Niven's, in his essay "Building The Mote in God's Eye", retold on Atomic Rockets and in reference wikis; the essay itself was not read for this dossier). The notional physics, as the wikis summarise it, is a fifth force generated by thermonuclear reactions, negligible in ordinary conditions, along whose equipotentials the tramlines form [LOW] S3. That is invented physics with a real engineer's fingerprints on it: the constraints (fixed points, fixed pairs, sublight legs, a delay after arrival) are the kind a designer adds to make a drive useful for stories about navies. No real proposal is cited and none descends from the book. The catalogue cousin for a fixed two-ended shortcut is Morris and Thorne 1988; the tramlines as natural lines between stars have the same role as the natural collapsars of The Forever War and the charted anomalies of Starman Jones, all three being ways to make the infrastructure pre-exist so that nobody has to build it.

Lineage: Physicists in the writers' room.

Which loophole, and why

L3, a shortcut with fixed endpoints. The ship is at one Alderson point and then at another, instantaneously; nothing crosses the space between and no other space is entered. The cousin is Morris and Thorne 1988, whose bill is negative energy at the throat scaling with its size, mass beyond engineering, a pre-laid route and a time machine once a mouth is moved. The Alderson drive pays the third by using natural tramlines, gives the throat no matter at all (the ship is not described as passing through anything) and never touches the first, second or fourth.

The rules it keeps

  • Points and tramlines are fixed by the stars. No jump except along an existing tramline from its Alderson point, and the points are far out [HIGH] S3.
  • Exactness. The ship must be at the point; the novel's navigation is a matter of reaching it precisely, and the return from the Mote is planned around one tramline whose far end is inside a star [HIGH] S3.
  • Jump shock. Crew and computers are useless for a time after every jump, without exception, and battles are timed to it [MED] S1.
  • The Field is separate. The Langston Field is a defensive device, not part of the drive, and having one without the other is the whole difference between the two species [HIGH] S3.
  • Sublight between jumps. Systems without a shared tramline are reached by chains of jumps and ordinary flight; distance still costs time [HIGH] S3.

F3: limitations with a stated shape (equipotential lines, fixed points, a post-jump delay), applied consistently through the novel and its sequel, with an engineer's hand visible in the constraints. Not F4, because the constraints are invented rather than real and the story's cost is tactical rather than physical; not F2, because the tramline rule is a claim about how the drive relates to the stars, not a bare cooldown.

What it ignores

A jump is instantaneous between systems light years apart, and the novel never says in what frame, never names a preferred frame and never asks what a jump followed by a jump back does to the order of events. The Alderson design as retold in secondary sources includes limits chosen to keep the drive from being a time machine, but this catalogue could not source what those limits were or whether the text states them [LOW] S3; on the evidence of the novel itself the question is never raised. Absence dated: as of 2026-09-12 neither The Mote in God's Eye nor The Gripping Hand (1993) addresses causality. C-silent. Energy, stability and tidal entries are untouched: the drive uses energy, the text says, and that is all.
