Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2 (2017): jump points

A galaxy threaded with fixed jump points, a rule that a body should take no more than fifty in a row, and a ship that takes seven hundred.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L3 · Notability: N3 · Coherence: F2 · Causality: C-silent · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

In the Marvel Cinematic Universe, ships get between stars through jump points: fixed places in space where a ship enters a portal and comes out somewhere far away. The first film (2014) uses them as scene changes. Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2 (2017), written and directed by James Gunn, is the instalment that states the rules, and it does so twice.

The first time is in the opening chase. Fleeing the Sovereign fleet, Quill asks how many jumps it is to the nearest habitable planet, Berhert. "Only one. But the access point is 47 clicks away", through a field of "quantum asteroids" (opening chase) [HIGH] S1. That exchange fixes the geometry: a jump is a discrete event, jumps are counted, and each starts at an access point you must reach at ordinary speed. The second time is the sequence the catalogue lists this film for. Rocket, Yondu, Kraglin and Groot escape the Ravager mutiny in the Quadrant, a detachable section of Yondu's ship, and Rocket sets course for Ego's planet. Yondu objects: "It ain't healthy for a mammalian body to hop more than 50 jumps at a time" (Yondu, Quadrant scene) [HIGH] S1. Rocket knows, and says they are about to do seven hundred. What follows is a montage of the Quadrant passing portal after portal while the occupants' faces and bodies stretch, distort and scream, until they drop out at Ego's planet, shaken but intact [HIGH] S1.

The film adds one more: jumps happen through a visible ring or portal that the ship flies into, and the far side is another such point. Nothing is said about who made the points, what they are or why there is one near Berhert and none nearer Ego. The count of seven hundred is offered as a measure of how far away Ego lives, "out past the edge of what's known", and the fifty-jump rule is the only physical cost of travel ever stated in the franchise [HIGH] S1. Later films keep the same picture, with jumps as portal transits and no new rules [MED] S1. The name "Universal Neural Teleportation Network" circulates on franchise wikis for the system; it is not spoken in the film and this dossier does not rely on it [LOW] S3.

Origin and lineage

James Gunn wrote and directed both Guardians films; the jump-point convention comes from the first film's visual language and from the comics' broader cosmic setting rather than from any named source [HIGH] S3. No real proposal is named in either film and no science consultation on the transport system is on record. The fifty-jump rule is Gunn's own invention, made for the seven-hundred-jump gag. The convention of fixed jump points reached by sublight flight is older than the films by decades in prose and in games: The Mote in God's Eye (1974) and Wing Commander (1990) both use it, and the catalogue carries each (the Alderson drive and Wing Commander are both catalogued); no direct borrowing by Gunn is claimed and none is sourced.

Which loophole, and why

L3, take a shortcut. Jumps run between fixed access points, each a portal in space, and a ship crosses the intervening distance by not crossing it; the clicks to the access point are flown at ordinary speed. There is no continuous fast transit and no other space that the ship visits, as far as the film shows; the seven-hundred-jump montage is a sequence of portals, not a tunnel. The catalogue's cousin is therefore Morris and Thorne 1988, a fixed traversable wormhole, and its bill: negative energy at the throat in amounts that grow with throat radius, mouths delivered in advance at sublight speed and a time machine on any relative motion of the mouths. The film's network pays the second in spirit, since nobody makes a jump point where they need one, and the other two not at all.

There is a case for noting a secondary L4 flavour, since the distortion of bodies in transit looks like passage through something with different rules. The film gives it no content, and the distortion is a cost of dose rather than of place.

The rules it keeps

  • Jumps are discrete and counted, and distance is measured in jumps [HIGH] S1.
  • Each jump starts at an access point, reached by ordinary flight over a stated distance [HIGH] S1.
  • A body should take no more than fifty jumps in a row; the film states the rule before breaking it, and shows the cost of breaking it as distortion and distress [HIGH] S1.
  • Anyone with a ship can use the network; there is no gatekeeper, no toll and no traffic [HIGH] S1.

These rules are consistent within the film and are used for two set pieces, the asteroid-field flight to the access point and the seven-hundred-jump montage. They carry no physics: what a jump point is, what it costs and why bodies suffer are never said. F2 by the grading scheme, rule-bound with no physical content. The fifty-jump rule is a limitation stated and applied, which is F3 territory, but it is a limitation on the passenger rather than on the drive, and the film uses it once, as a joke with consequences.

What it ignores

Time. Seven hundred jumps take the Quadrant across a distance the film describes as beyond the known, in a sequence of hours at most by the characters' clocks, and no instalment asks whether the far end is "now" for anyone, or whether a ship jumping in one direction and back could meet itself. The franchise does time travel elsewhere, in Avengers: Endgame, by a different mechanism with its own stated rules, and never through a jump [MED] S1. C-silent.

The register would also ask what holds seven hundred portals open, who laid them and what the distortion of a body in transit is a symptom of. None is engaged. The one cost the film states, the fifty-jump limit, is a rule about dose and it is never connected to anything about the points themselves.
