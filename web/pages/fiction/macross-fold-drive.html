Super Dimension Fortress Macross (1982): the space fold

An alien fold system in a rebuilt warship, fired once in a panic near the Earth's surface, that takes an island along, lands the ship at Pluto and then is not there any more.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L3 · Notability: N3 · Coherence: F2 · Causality: C-silent · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

Super Dimension Fortress Macross (Studio Nue and Tatsunoko, 36 episodes, broadcast from October 1982) is built on a single early misuse of its drive. The Macross itself is an alien warship that crashed on Earth in 1999 and was rebuilt over a decade using its own "overtechnology"; its fold system, the mechanism for a space fold, is one of the things nobody has been able to test [HIGH] S1. Episode 3, "Space Fold" (broadcast 17 October 1982), is where it is used. Under attack by the Zentradi fleet and having lost his escort carriers, Captain Global orders a fold to the far side of the Moon and, to deceive the enemy, has the ship feint towards its island base and fold from just above it [HIGH] S1. The fold takes the ship and a sphere of its surroundings, South Ataria Island and the sea around it, and when the ship comes out it is not behind the Moon but in the orbit of Pluto [HIGH] S1. The Zentradi commanders watching from orbit are shocked that anyone would fold so close to a planet's surface. Global assumes the ship can fold back; the engine room reports that the fold system has disappeared, and the captain lights his pipe and says it is going to be a long trip [HIGH] S1. The rest of the series is that trip, at ordinary speed, with the island's civilians rebuilding their town inside the ship.

That is the entire definition of the drive in the original series. A fold is a jump: the ship vanishes and reappears elsewhere, with no transit shown from inside. It is imprecise, at least when fired near a gravity well, by a factor the show measures in the difference between the Moon and Pluto. It carries a region of space with it, whose size is not given. And the drive can be consumed or lost by its own use, in a way the show never explains. The Zentradi use folds routinely and accurately, which establishes that the trouble is human inexperience rather than the technology [HIGH] S1.

The franchise elaborated later. Macross: Do You Remember Love? (1984) restages the fold; Macross Plus (1994) and Macross 7 (1994) add fold boosters as consumable units; Macross Frontier (2008) introduces "fold faults", regions in which fold travel is slowed or blocked so that a fold can take days rather than minutes, and "fold quartz" as the material that makes the drive work [MED] S3. None of this is in the 1982 series and this dossier scores the series, where the drive is defined by its failure.

Origin and lineage

Created by Shōji Kawamori and Studio Nue with Haruhiko Mikimoto's designs and Noboru Ishiguro directing, the series was made to sell transforming toys and became something else [HIGH] S3. The name is the tell: "super dimension" (chōjikū) is the series' word for the space a fold passes through, and it is a name rather than a mechanism. No real proposal is cited; the fold predates the traversable-wormhole literature by six years and the term "fold space" belongs to the same family as Herbert's folding of space in Dune (1965), which the catalogue treats separately. No direct borrowing is sourced and none is asserted. The series reached English-speaking audiences as the first third of Robotech (1985), which kept the Pluto fold intact [HIGH] S3.

No forward influence on physics is claimed. The fold's influence is on anime and on games: nearly every Japanese space opera since has a fold, a warp or both.

Which loophole, and why

L3, take a shortcut, with an L4 name. A fold is a discrete jump from one place to another with no fast transit through ordinary space; the ship is here, then it is there. The show's own metaphor is folding, which is the L3 picture in the catalogue's sense, two distant points brought together. The "super dimension" the fold passes through is L4 vocabulary and the series gives it no content in 1982; the primary class is L3 by what is shown.

The cousin is Morris and Thorne 1988, the traversable wormhole, and its bill is what an on-demand fold would have to pay: negative energy at the throat scaling with the size of what passes (the fold in episode 3 carries an island, so the throat is kilometres across), a far mouth laid in advance at sublight speed (the fold has none, which is CON-2's objection in one line) and a time machine on any round trip of the mouths. The Macross fold pays none of these on screen. What it pays instead is accuracy, and the show says so: the fold system did not do what was asked and then was gone.

The rules it keeps

  • A fold is a jump: departure, no transit, arrival [HIGH] S1.
  • The fold takes a region of space with it, and folding near a planet's surface is dangerous and imprecise; the Zentradi say so and the Pluto arrival shows it [HIGH] S1.
  • The drive can be lost by its use; after episode 3 the Macross has no fold system and the series holds to that, at ordinary speed, for the rest of the voyage home [HIGH] S1.
  • The technology is alien and understood by its makers: Zentradi folds are accurate and routine, human folds are not [HIGH] S1.

These rules are consistent within the series and one of them structures the whole show. They have no physical content: no energy, no mechanism, no explanation of what "super dimension" is or why the fold system vanished. F2 by the grading scheme. The later franchise's fold faults are a stated limitation applied consistently and would lift Frontier to F3 on its own; the 1982 series is scored as it stands.

What it ignores

Time. A fold from Earth to Pluto's orbit, five light-hours out, is a time advance over the light path by any reckoning, and the series does not ask whose clocks agree afterwards; the ship's calendar and Earth's are treated as the same throughout the voyage home [HIGH] S1. No frame is named, no rule is stated, no consequence is drawn. C-silent. Frontier's fold faults, in which a fold can take days, are a later step towards taking time seriously, but they concern delay rather than causality and are outside this dossier's scope.

The register's other questions, what holds a fold open, what the fold system was and why it vanished, what an island-sized fold costs, are unasked. Absence is the series' method: the drive's one use is a disaster, after which the story is about living with the consequence rather than about the machine.
