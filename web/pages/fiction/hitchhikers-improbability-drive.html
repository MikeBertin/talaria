The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy: the Infinite Improbability Drive and bistromathics

Douglas Adams's starship drives, from the 1978 radio series and 1979 novel onward: a ship that is everywhere at once because that is very unlikely, and a later one steered by restaurant arithmetic.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L4 · Notability: N1 · Coherence: F0 · Causality: C-owns · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

The Heart of Gold, the ship stolen by Zaphod Beeblebrox, is powered by the Infinite Improbability Drive. It first appears in the radio series, in "Fit the Second", broadcast on BBC Radio 4 on 15 March 1978, where Ford Prefect and Arthur Dent, thrown out of a Vogon airlock, are picked up by it against odds the ship's computer reads aloud as they fall [HIGH] S3. The novel of 1979 keeps the scene: chapter 9 has the improbability factor counting down from "two to the power of one hundred thousand to one against and falling" as Ford briefly becomes a penguin and Arthur's limbs start to detach, and ends with the rescued pair being told that an infinite number of monkeys outside want to discuss their script for Hamlet [HIGH] S1. Chapter 10 is the Guide's own entry on the drive, and it is the closest thing the work has to a definition [HIGH] S1.

That entry says the drive is a method of crossing vast interstellar distances in "a mere nothingth of a second", and does so, in Adams's phrase, "without all that tedious mucking about in hyperspace" [HIGH] S1 (chapter 10). The mechanism, as the Guide tells it, is that small amounts of finite improbability could already be generated by hooking a Bambleweeny 57 Sub-Meson Brain to an atomic vector plotter suspended in a strong Brownian motion producer, for which a nice hot cup of tea will do; the trick was mainly used to break the ice at parties. A student at the Galactic Government's research centre on Damogran reasoned that if an infinite improbability generator was a virtual impossibility it must be a finite improbability, fed that figure into a finite improbability generator, and made one, after which a mob of respectable physicists lynched him [HIGH] S1 (chapter 10). The radio and novel versions both have the ship pass through every conceivable point in every conceivable universe while the drive is at infinite improbability, and the ship's passengers see whatever is least likely: a whale and a bowl of petunias called into being above Magrathea are the famous case [MED] S3. What the drive does not do, anywhere in the work, is move the ship through space in any describable way; the ship is simply somewhere else when normality is restored, and the computer's own announcement is that normality will be restored "just as soon as we are sure what is normal anyway" [HIGH] S1 (chapter 9).

Hyperspace exists too, and is the ordinary way everybody else travels. In chapter 6 the Vogon constructor fleet jumps to hyperspace for the journey to Barnard's Star, Ford warns Arthur that the jump is "unpleasantly like being drunk", and when Arthur asks what is so unpleasant about being drunk Ford tells him to ask a glass of water [HIGH] S1 (chapter 6). The improbability drive is the joke told against that convention, and the convention is what makes it land.

Adams added a second drive in Life, the Universe and Everything (1982). Slartibartfast's ship, the Bistromath, is fitted out as an Italian restaurant and runs on bistromathics, "a revolutionary new way of understanding the behaviour of numbers", introduced in chapter 7 in most printings [MED] S3. The novel's argument is that just as Einstein found space and time not absolute, numbers are now known to depend on "the observer's movement in restaurants": the number of people a table is booked for, the number who turn up and the figures on the bill obey no known arithmetic, and a computer that exploits this can take a ship anywhere. The novel's Guide entry says the drive crosses interstellar distances without all the dangerous mucking about with improbability factors that the earlier drive required [MED] S3. The Bistromath also travels in time: it collects Arthur and Ford at Lord's cricket ground, to which they have just arrived from prehistoric Earth, and carries them into the Krikkit affair across deep time [MED] S3.

Origin and lineage

Adams wrote the radio series for BBC Radio 4 in 1977 and 1978, with the first series of six episodes ("Fits") broadcast from 8 March 1978, and turned the first four into the novel published by Pan in October 1979 [HIGH] S3. The drive was written as a parody of the hyperspace drives of pulp and television science fiction, and Adams's own gloss on it, in the Guide entries, is a parody of the pop-science voice that explains such drives; the physics being sent up is the loophole itself, the habit of inventing a space where the rules are different and then declining to say what the rules are. The Bistromath is a second telling of the same joke: a drive whose named principle is nonsense and says so.

No real proposal is named in the work and no real proposal fed it. The Brownian motion producer is the one piece of real physics invoked, as a joke: Brownian motion is the random jiggling of particles in a fluid, first explained by Einstein in 1905, and a cup of tea does exhibit it. Adams borrowed the name "improbability" from nothing in the literature; the drive's ancestry is comic, not physical, and this catalogue asserts no influence in either direction. The drive did feed language: "infinite improbability" became a common tag for hand-waved FTL in later commentary, and the tea-powered generator turns up as a reference in physics outreach writing about probability [LOW] S4.

Which loophole, and why

L4, leave the manifold, because the drive's one stated property is that the ship is at every point at once and then at the chosen one, which is a statement about not being in ordinary space rather than about moving through it. The Guide entry defines the drive against hyperspace, the L4 archetype, and the ship's alternative to it is a different way of not being anywhere. Nothing in the work claims the ship moves fast (L1), that space is moved around it (L2) or that two points are joined (L3). The Bistromath is the same class: numbers in a restaurant obey a different arithmetic, and the ship goes where the arithmetic says, which is an other-space argument with the space replaced by a menu.

The physics cousins are the two dossiers on extra dimensions. The Kaluza-Klein hyperspace reading is the negative finding that the extra dimensions physics knows about are compact or warped, share the light cone of ordinary space and give no separate space with laxer rules; its bill is B-new and the finding that "hyperspace" as fiction uses the word has no counterpart. Brane-bulk shortcuts is the one real shortcut through a bulk: a graviton can leave a bent brane and return before light along it, at one part in $10^{58}$ today, positive-energy by construction and still, to anyone on the brane, a signal to the past. Its bill is B-new, B-mass and B-caus. The register entry that governs the whole class is MAN-1. The join is that Adams's drive does, as a joke, exactly what the physics says a real extra dimension cannot do: give a second space in which "here" and "there" are the same place. The improbability drive is not a proposal, so it pays no bill; the point of filing it is that its punchline is the short summary of the class.

The rules it keeps

The drive is a joke and the work says so on every page it appears, which is what makes it a perfect F0 under GRADING.md: the drive is the punchline and knows it. Its consistency is comic consistency. The improbability factor is always announced, always absurd and always falls to normality; the side effects are always the least likely thing available (a penguin, a whale, a sperm whale's brief and touching inner monologue); the ship goes where the plot needs it and the text says as much by making "where the plot needs it" the drive's operating principle. That principle is applied without exception through the radio series and the first two novels, and when Adams wanted a new joke he did not modify the old drive, he built a second one on a different absurdity. Bistromathics keeps its own rule too: every statement about the drive is a true statement about restaurant bills.

Two things are kept straight that a lesser comic drive would not bother with. First, the improbability drive is distinguished from hyperspace, which the Vogons use in the ordinary way, so the work has a conventional FTL baseline against which the joke reads. Second, the counting-down improbability factor is a real narrative constraint inside the scene: things get less strange as the number falls, and Adams times the jokes to it.

What it ignores

Everything the register would ask, deliberately. The work never says what the drive costs in energy, what the ship is doing while it is at every point in every universe, or why it comes back to one. It never says whether "a mere nothingth of a second" is measured on the ship, at Damogran or by the whale. It never addresses what an FTL arrival does to the ordering of events for observers who did not make the trip.

On causality the mark is C-owns, on the following argument. The work does not link FTL to time travel through the improbability drive itself, but it admits the implication of playing fast and loose with spacetime and uses it relentlessly: the second novel's Milliways is a restaurant at the end of the universe reached by time travel, and the Guide's entry on the subject says the major problem of time travel is one of grammar, for which Dr Dan Streetmentioner's Time Traveller's Handbook of 1001 Tense Formations exists [MED] S1 (The Restaurant at the End of the Universe, 1980, the Milliways chapters). The third novel's Bistromath moves its passengers through time as easily as through space [MED] S3. Adams treats FTL and time travel as one continuum and jokes about the paradoxes, which is what owning them looks like in a comedy. The drive never picks a frame and never pretends to; that would be a rule, and the work has none. Last checked 2026-09-12 against the radio scripts, the first three novels and the 2005 film: no instalment addresses the causal cost of the drive.
