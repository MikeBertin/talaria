Star Trek: transwarp, quantum slipstream and the other beyond-warp drives

Everything in Star Trek that goes faster than warp: Borg conduits through transwarp space, the infinite-velocity threshold, the quantum slipstream and the mushroom network, each introduced when the plot needed a faster ship and each retired when it did not.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L4 · Notability: N2 · Coherence: F1 · Causality: C-silent · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

"Transwarp" is a label, not a mechanism; the franchise attaches it to at least four unrelated things, and two further drives do the same job under other names. Each is defined in one instalment.

The Excelsior's transwarp drive. Star Trek III: The Search for Spock (1984) introduces the USS Excelsior as the Great Experiment, a ship with a transwarp drive that is meant to outrun the Enterprise and fails to engage because Scott has sabotaged it [HIGH] S1. Nothing is said about how it would have worked; the film later treats the Excelsior as an ordinary warp ship.

Borg transwarp conduits. "Descent" (The Next Generation, 1993) has a Borg ship open a conduit and the Enterprise-D follow it; La Forge likens the entry to falling into a fast river and being swept along, and computes from the distance covered that the conduit was about twenty times faster than warp 9.8 [HIGH] S1. "Dark Frontier" (Voyager, 1999) makes the conduits infrastructure: a transwarp coil taken from a Borg sphere lets Voyager open a conduit and cross twenty thousand light years in days [HIGH] S1. "Endgame" (Voyager, 2001) shows the network's hub, a nebula-hidden structure whose conduits reach every quadrant; Voyager rides one thirty thousand light years to Earth in minutes while the hub is destroyed behind it [HIGH] S1. Later series reuse the abandoned conduits as shortcuts (Picard, Discovery).

The transwarp threshold. "Threshold" (Voyager 2x15, 1996) is the franchise's one attempt to say what lies past warp 10. A new form of dilithium lets Tom Paris fly the shuttle Cochrane to warp 10, defined as infinite velocity: the vessel occupies every point in the universe simultaneously, and the pilot returns with sensor data from everywhere and begins to mutate. The episode ends with Paris and Janeway devolved into amphibians and the technology abandoned [HIGH] S1. The franchise has not used it since.

Quantum slipstream. "Hope and Fear" (Voyager, 1998) introduces the USS Dauntless, a fake Starfleet ship built by the alien Arturis with a drive that routes energy through the main deflector to focus a quantum field, penetrates a quantum barrier and rides a slipstream; the phase variance of the field must be continually corrected or the slipstream collapses and throws the ship out. Voyager rigs its own, follows for an hour, covers three hundred light years and concludes it cannot safely be used again [HIGH] S1. "Timeless" (1998) has the crew build a slipstream drive from benamite crystals, lose the phase variance, and crash on an ice planet; fifteen years later Kim and Chakotay send corrected phase data into the past through a stolen Borg temporal transmitter, and the second attempt drops Voyager safely out of the slipstream ten years closer to home [HIGH] S1. Slipstream returns as a working Starfleet drive in Prodigy (2022 onward) [MED] S3.

Coaxial warp drive. "Vis à Vis" (Voyager, 1998) presents a drive that folds space so that the ship arrives without crossing the distance, which the episode treats as theoretical for Starfleet [MED] S3.

The spore drive. Discovery (2017) defines the displacement-activated spore hub drive in "Context Is for Kings": a mycelial network spans the cosmos, spores of Prototaxites stellaviatori let a ship jump along it, and during the jump the ship is in the mycelial plane rather than in normal space. Lorca's line is the definition: "An infinite number of roads leading everywhere" [HIGH] S1. The USS Glenn jumps ninety light years in 1.3 seconds and its crew are killed on exit by a Hawking-radiation firewall; navigation needs a living interface, first a tardigrade and then Stamets himself; Starfleet builds the drive at scale, decommissions it after the war and later revives it for a jump of 51,450 light years [HIGH] S1 for the episodes, [HIGH] S3 for the figures as tabulated by Memory Alpha.

Origin and lineage

Each drive has its own authors: Harve Bennett for the film, Ronald D. Moore and others for "Descent", Brannon Braga for "Threshold", Rick Berman and Braga for "Hope and Fear", Bryan Fuller and the Discovery writers' room for the spore drive [MED] S3. The slipstream has a documented afterlife: Memory Alpha notes that Gene Roddenberry's Andromeda (2000) made slipstream its primary drive, and the catalogue records it at Andromeda slipstream. No real proposal is named or credited by any of these episodes; the spore drive's "Hawking radiation firewall" borrows two real phrases (Hawking radiation, and the black-hole firewall of Almheiri, Marolf, Polchinski and Sully, 2012) for an effect that is neither. Nothing in physics descends from any of them.

Which loophole, and why

L4 primary, with L1 and L3 members. The Borg conduits run through transwarp space, the spore drive through the mycelial plane, the slipstream through a quantum barrier: three names for another space with laxer rules, entered and left, which is the definition of L4. The physics cousins are Kaluza-Klein and the hyperspace reading and brane-bulk shortcuts. Their bill is the register's MAN-1: extra dimensions in physics are compact or warped, share the light cone of ordinary space and offer no domain with different rules; the one real shortcut through a bulk is positive-energy but of order $10^{-58}$ today, and looks to a brane observer like ordinary FTL with all of CAU-1's consequences unless the bulk supplies a preferred frame.

The transwarp threshold is L1 taken to its limit: a ship in ordinary space at infinite speed, occupying every point at once, which is not a speed but a claim about a spacelike hypersurface, and the cousin is LOR-1 with no escape. The coaxial drive and the conduit hub are L3, shortcuts between fixed points, priced against Morris and Thorne 1988: exotic matter at the throat scaling with its size. The slipstream's deflector-focused field could be read as L2 and priced against Alcubierre 1994, but the episodes say the ship enters a slipstream, not that space is moved around it.

The rules it keeps

Few, and none for long, which is why the mark is F1 rather than F2. The Borg conduit network is the most consistent: conduits have apertures, coils open them, the hub is a fixed structure and destroying it closes the network [HIGH] S1. The slipstream has one stated rule, the phase variance, applied in both episodes [HIGH] S1. The spore drive has two, the need for a living navigator and the requirement to be out of warp or at rest for a jump, both applied across Discovery [HIGH] S1. But each drive is introduced when a faster ship is needed, is limited by a reason that applies only to that episode, and is retired or forgotten: the Excelsior drive is never mentioned again, the threshold is disowned, the slipstream cannot be risked and is then risked, coaxial warp is a one-off, and the spore drive is decommissioned by fiat at the end of a season. That is the definition of F1: the drive works when the plot needs it to.

What it ignores

The frame question, entirely. None of these drives is ever considered as a signal to the past; the single Starfleet clock is assumed. The mark needs one qualification. "Timeless" does send information into the past, the phase corrections that save the ship, but by a separate Borg temporal transmitter and not by the slipstream, and the episode treats the paradox by having the new past replace the old with no comment on consistency [HIGH] S1. "Threshold" has a ship occupying all points at once, which is a spacelike surface of simultaneity in some frame, and asks nothing about which frame. C-silent, dated 2026-09-12 across the film, TNG, DS9, Voyager, Discovery, Picard and Prodigy.
