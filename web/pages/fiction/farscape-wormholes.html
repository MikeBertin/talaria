Farscape (1999): wormholes, and starburst as a separate thing

A show that begins with an astronaut falling down a wormhole and ends with him proving that a wormhole is a weapon; in between it says out loud that every wormhole is also a road into the past.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L3 · Notability: N3 · Coherence: F4 · Causality: C-owns · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

Farscape (Jim Henson Company and Nine Network, 1999 to 2003, plus the 2004 miniseries The Peacekeeper Wars) has two ways of covering distance and keeps them apart. The one the series is about is the wormhole. The one the ship uses is starburst.

The wormhole is defined in the pilot, "Premiere" (1.01). IASA astronaut John Crichton flies the module Farscape-1 to test a theory that a spacecraft can use a planet's gravity and atmosphere to reach unprecedented speed; at the moment of "critical apogee" an electromagnetic wave arrives, a wormhole opens in front of him and he is flung to "some distant part of the universe" [HIGH] S1. He spends the series trying to get home, and the wormhole knowledge that would let him is placed in his head by the Ancients, a species that used wormholes to leave the known universe, in "A Human Reaction" (1.16) [HIGH] S1. Others want that knowledge for a different reason. Scorpius, then the Peacekeeper command and the Scarran Hierarchy, want wormholes as weapons able to destroy a ship, a planet or a star system ("Infinite Possibilities", 3.14 and 3.15) [HIGH] S1. The miniseries ends the argument: Crichton opens a wormhole weapon before the combined fleets, a self-sustaining wormhole that would consume the galaxy, and uses the demonstration to force a peace [HIGH] S1.

The physics is stated in "Unrealized Reality" (4.11, written by David Kemper), which is the episode this dossier is built on. Crichton is pulled through a wormhole into the private realm of one of the Ancients' kind, whom he names Einstein. Einstein tells him that space and time are fused, that movement through space at speed becomes movement through time and that wormholes bridge space-time; that every wormhole system has millions of exits, each to a distinct time and place; and that if you travel from A to B and try to return, you may arrive at A immediately after you left, a cycle later, a cycle earlier or ten thousand cycles either way [HIGH] S1. Unskilled travel creates "unrealized realities", branches in which things went differently, several of which Crichton is dropped into during the episode. Navigation is "not a science, it's an art": the traveller must hold the space-time signature of the destination in mind, and "destination is the key" [HIGH] S1. The next two episodes cash the warning. In "Kansas" (4.12) Crichton reaches Earth and finds it is the 1980s, with the timeline already altered so that his father is now scheduled for a shuttle flight that will kill him, and the crew must put history back before returning through the wormhole; "Terra Firma" (4.13) brings him to the Earth of his own time [HIGH] S1.

Starburst is the other thing. Moya, the living ship, is a Leviathan, and starburst is her own faculty: she opens a rift of light and is gone, with a recharge time and low energy reserves as the usual constraint, and where she comes out is imprecise [HIGH] S1 ("Premiere", where Zhaan explains that Moya cannot starburst again because her reserves are low). The show never gives starburst a physics; the fan-wiki account of it as travel through a one-dimensional space is a rationalisation and is not the show's statement [LOW] S3. Peacekeeper ships use "hetch drive", a conventional engine with a unit of speed attached, and whether hetch speeds exceed light is never said [HIGH] S1. Starburst is scored below as the series depicts it, a black box, and is one reason the dossier's grade is for the wormholes and not for the ship.

Origin and lineage

Created by Rockne S. O'Bannon and produced by Brian Henson and David Kemper, Farscape was written in Australia and the United States between 1998 and 2003 [HIGH] S3. The wormhole is the series' founding image: an astronaut, a gravity-assist experiment and a hole in space, in that order. No real paper is named on screen, but the show's vocabulary is the physics vocabulary of its decade: "wormhole" rather than "warp" or "hyperspace", space-time signatures, the fusion of space and time, and a character called Einstein to say so [HIGH] S1. The idea that a wormhole connects times as well as places, and that a careless return can land you before you left, is Morris, Thorne and Yurtsever put into a script; no writer interview sourcing that borrowing has been found for this dossier, and it is not asserted as one.

The forward influence is on television rather than on physics. Farscape's wormhole weapon, a hole that eats a galaxy, is the strongest statement in popular fiction that a wormhole's danger is not travel but what it does to the space around it; no paper cites it.

Lineage: The wormhole goes to television.

Which loophole, and why

L3, take a shortcut. A wormhole in Farscape is a tunnel with an entrance and millions of exits, entered by flying a module or a ship into it, and it delivers the traveller elsewhere without local superluminal motion. The show's own word is "wormhole" throughout, and Einstein's account of it as a bridge in space-time is the Morris–Thorne picture in plain words.

The cousin is Morris and Thorne 1988, with the time-machine sequel as the specific join. The bill in those dossiers is negative energy at the throat at neutron-star-core pressures, a far mouth carried at sublight speed before the first trip, and conversion to a time machine by any relative motion of the mouths. Farscape pays the last item in full and on purpose: its wormholes are time machines, that is the plot, and the show's answer to the danger is that the knowledge must be suppressed, which is a fictional CAU-2, chronology protection by policy rather than by back reaction.

Starburst, if scored alone, would be L4 by shape (the ship leaves and reappears) and F1 by content. The series keeps it separate and so does this dossier.

The rules it keeps

  • Wormholes connect points in time as well as space, and a return trip can arrive before departure. Stated by Einstein in "Unrealized Reality" and enacted in "Kansas" [HIGH] S1.
  • Careless travel branches history. The unrealized realities of 4.11 and the altered 1980s of 4.12 are consequences, and the fix in 4.12 is to repair the first change so that the rest "falls back into place" [HIGH] S1.
  • Navigation requires the traveller, not the ship: the space-time signature of the destination must be held in mind, which is why the knowledge in Crichton's head matters and why the Peacekeepers cannot simply build one [HIGH] S1.
  • Wormholes are dangerous to bodies and hulls. Peacekeeper attempts to send Prowlers through end with liquefied pilots; Crichton's module survives because it was built for it and because he knows the way [MED] S3 (episode references via the Farscape Encyclopedia Project; the liquefaction is shown in season 3).
  • The same knowledge that opens a wormhole for travel opens one as a weapon, and the weapon is unbounded: the miniseries shows a wormhole that would eat the galaxy [HIGH] S1.
  • Starburst is Moya's, has a recharge time, drains her reserves and is imprecise in destination; it is never a wormhole and never engineered [HIGH] S1.

This is F4 by the grading scheme: the work names a real constraint, that a bridge through space-time is a bridge through time, and builds story around its cost, from the fourth season's structure to the ending of the whole series. It is not the tidiest F4 in the catalogue. Unrealized realities are metaphysics the register has no row for, the weapon is a fantasy of scale, and starburst is a handwave left untouched beside it. The mark is for what the wormholes do, which is more than any other television series of the period asked of them.

What it ignores

Very little of the causality question, which is why the mark is C-owns. The show states the implication in words (Einstein), shows it (Crichton in the 1980s), prices it (unrealized realities, the need to hold the destination in mind, the Ancients' policy of suppression) and makes the finale turn on it. What it never does is ask about frames: there is no preferred frame, no rule that jumps are simultaneous for everyone, and no attempt to say why a return that lands "a cycle earlier" is possible at all beyond "it's not a science, it's an art" [HIGH] S1. The show owns the paradox by letting it happen and by treating the knowledge as too dangerous to hold.

The register's other questions go unanswered. What holds a wormhole open, what a wormhole is made of, what the weapon's energy source is and why a naturally occurring wormhole system should have millions of exits are never touched. Starburst and hetch drive are never priced in any way. Absence here is confined to the engineering; the show's silence is on cost, not on consequence.
