Asimov's Foundation: the Jump through hyperspace (1942 onward)

The Jump as a solved problem: a region that is neither space nor time, crossed between two instants, with the work done beforehand at a calculating machine.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L4 · Notability: N1 · Coherence: F3 · Causality: C-dodges · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

The drive is defined on the first pages of Foundation (1951), in the opening section "The Psychohistorians", as the young mathematician Gaal Dornick travels to Trantor. Asimov states the light barrier and the loophole in one paragraph. Travel through ordinary space cannot go faster than light, which would mean years between even neighbouring inhabited systems, and so the Jump remains "the only practical method of travelling between the stars"; through hyperspace, "neither space nor time, matter nor energy, something nor nothing", one can cross the Galaxy "in the interval between two neighboring instants of time" [HIGH] S1 (Foundation, Part I, chapter 1; the passage as reproduced in the Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction and at technovelgy). What the passenger feels is a trifling jar, a small internal kick that ends before he is sure he felt it [HIGH] S1. The ships are "hyperatomic"; the engine and the mathematics are never described, and Gaal's whole experience of interstellar travel is waiting for the next Jump and noticing when it has happened.

That opening section was written for the 1951 book; the four stories that follow it appeared in Astounding from May 1942 and take the Jump for granted, with traders and warships moving about the Galaxy and the emphasis always on what happens at either end [HIGH] S3 (Wikipedia, Foundation, publication history). Across the original trilogy the recurring practical rule is that a Jump has to be computed before it is made, and that the computation takes time: ships wait in normal space while the course for the next Jump is worked out, and a long trip is a chain of Jumps with intervals between them [MED] S1 (Foundation and Empire, 1952, and Second Foundation, 1953, from memory of the voyages of Toran and Bayta and of Arkady Darell; not rechecked against the text this session). In Foundation's Edge (1982) the sequels modernise the machinery: Trevize's ship the Far Star has a gravitic drive and a computer that calculates a Jump in moments and can run a series of them, where older ships needed days between Jumps and a safe distance from any large mass before making one [MED] S1 (Foundation's Edge, from memory of the early chapters; Wikipedia confirms only the gravitic ship and the 1982 date). The Jump also carries the series' communication: the hyperwave relay, which lets messages cross the Galaxy at Jump speed.

Asimov wrote one story that prices the Jump, and it is not in the Foundation series. In "Escape!" (1945, collected in I, Robot, 1950) the calculating machine the Brain designs a hyperatomic ship, and the humans who ride it cease to exist for the duration of the jump and return, which the Brain has decided is a joke rather than a death [HIGH] S1 (from memory of the story; stable and well known).

Origin and lineage

Isaac Asimov wrote the first Foundation story for John W. Campbell's Astounding Science Fiction in 1942, and Campbell was the editor who had put the word hyperspace into space travel eleven years earlier (Campbell 1931) [HIGH] S2 (Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction, entry "hyperspace", citing Campbell 1931 and Asimov 1951). Asimov's usage, and the shape of the Jump (instantaneous, computed, felt as a jar), became the pulp default and then the general one; the Historical Dictionary's citation trail runs from Campbell through E. E. Smith (1939) and Sturgeon (1941) to Asimov (1951) and on to 2022 [HIGH] S2. No physics proposal is named in the series, and Asimov, who was a biochemist by training, never claimed one. The lineage page planned for this line is hyperspace-the-word (see lineage).

Which loophole, and why

L4, leave the manifold. Asimov's own words settle it: the ship passes through a region that is not space, and normal space is where the ship is between Jumps. There is no tunnel, no carried bubble and no faster object; the rule that nothing in ordinary space beats light is stated in the same paragraph as the loophole. A secondary L5 exists in the hyperwave relay, which is the same mechanism for messages, and is not scored separately here.

The physics cousin is Kaluza-Klein and the hyperspace reading, the register's MAN-1 worked through. Extra dimensions in physics are compact or warped, a point in them is a point of the same spacetime with another coordinate and ordinary matter cannot leave the brane; "neither space nor time" has no counterpart. The nearest physics to an instantaneous Jump is the brane-bulk shortcut, whose bill is a new warped dimension, energy densities of the early universe to make the gain noticeable and CAU-1's antitelephone unless the bulk supplies a frame. Asimov's Galaxy has the frame and none of the rest.

The rules it keeps

  • Nothing in ordinary space exceeds light. Stated in the defining passage and never broken: the ships accelerate normally between Jumps [HIGH] S1.
  • The Jump is instantaneous in Galactic time. "Between two neighboring instants" in 1951 and the same in 1982 [HIGH] S1.
  • A Jump must be computed first, and the computation is the bottleneck: hours in the trilogy, moments with the Far Star's computer, which is presented as an advance and not a change of physics [MED] S1.
  • Distance from masses before a Jump, stated in Foundation's Edge as the older constraint that gravitic ships relax [MED] S1.
  • Long trips are chains of Jumps, each landing the ship in normal space [MED] S1.

The mark is F3. The work names a real limitation (the light barrier), states its loophole in one sentence and applies the loophole's own rules consistently for forty years of instalments. It does not build story around the cost of the physics, which is what F4 asks; the cost of a Jump in the Foundation series is a calculation, and calculation is what the series is about.

What it ignores

The register's first question is never asked. A Jump is instantaneous "for everyone": the Galaxy keeps one calendar (the Galactic Era) and the interval between two instants is the same interval on Trantor and on Terminus. That is a preferred frame in the sense of CAU-4, and the mark is C-dodges on the strength of the stated rule, not of any argument in the text: no instalment mentions that a Jump in one frame is a trip to the past in another, and no character ever receives a hyperwave message before it was sent. The rule answers the constraint without naming it. The register would also ask what a Jump spends, what hyperspace is made of and why masses disturb it; the series says nothing on any of them as of 2026-09-12. Asimov's one engagement with the Jump's cost to the traveller is in "Escape!" and is played as a joke.
