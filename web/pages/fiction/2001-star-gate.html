2001: A Space Odyssey (1968): the Star Gate

A slab left on a moon of Saturn that opens into a switching station for the galaxy; Clarke's novel and Kubrick's film show the passage and refuse to explain it.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L3 · Notability: N1 · Coherence: F2 · Causality: C-silent · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

The Star Gate is the second half of the monolith. Arthur C. Clarke's novel and Stanley Kubrick's film were written together in 1964 to 1968, and they diverge in where the gate is and how much is said about it, so both are described here.

In the novel, the Tycho monolith's radio burst is aimed at Japetus, a moon of Saturn (Clarke's spelling), and Discovery is sent there [HIGH] S1. On the white plain of Japetus Bowman finds a monolith two kilometres tall, which the novel calls Big Brother and then, as he descends towards it in the pod, the Star Gate [HIGH] S1. His last transmission to Earth is the line that everybody knows, ending "it's full of stars!" (chapter 39, "Into the Eye") [HIGH] S1. Chapter 40, "Exit", has the gate open and close; chapter 41 is titled "Grand Central", because what Bowman sees on the far side is a vast enclosed space through which other things are passing, and which he reads as a switching station, a junction on a network of gates [HIGH] S1. He is carried on through an alien sky, close by a red giant and its white dwarf companion, and set down in a hotel room built from television broadcasts (chapters 42 to 44), where he sleeps, ages, is remade as the Star-Child and returns to Earth's orbit [HIGH] S1. The passage is a passage: Bowman is inside the pod throughout, does nothing to steer and is delivered.

In the film, the destination is Jupiter, not Saturn, because Kubrick and Douglas Trumbull could not make rings they were satisfied with [HIGH] S3. The final act is titled "Jupiter and Beyond the Infinite"; the monolith floats in orbit among the moons; Bowman's pod approaches it and the screen becomes the corridor of light that Trumbull built with slit-scan photography, then landscapes in false colour, then the hotel room [HIGH] S1. The film has no narration and no dialogue after HAL is shut down, so the film's Star Gate is defined entirely by what is shown: a slab, a pod, a passage, a room, a rebirth. The name "Star Gate" is the production's, used by Trumbull and Clarke, and is never spoken in the film [HIGH] S3.

Two things are constant across both versions. The gate is alien infrastructure, not a drive: nobody builds it, nobody carries it, it is found where it was left three million years earlier, and it is one node of a system. And the work declines to explain it. Clarke's chapter 37, "Experiment", gives the makers' history, minds that outgrew bodies and learned to store themselves in the structure of space itself, and stops there [HIGH] S1. That refusal is a choice, and it is the reason this dossier is F2 rather than F1: the gate has consistent rules and no stated mechanism, and the work says so.

Origin and lineage

Clarke's seed is "The Sentinel", a 1951 short story about an artefact left on the Moon as a tripwire for a species that could reach it [HIGH] S3. Kubrick approached Clarke in 1964; the novel was written alongside the screenplay and follows earlier drafts of the film, which is why the book has Saturn and the film Jupiter [HIGH] S3 (Wikipedia, citing Clarke's own account). Clarke's The Lost Worlds of 2001 (1972) prints discarded drafts and shows how late the gate took its final shape [MED] S3. Clarke kept Jupiter in 2010: Odyssey Two (1982), where the monolith is shown to be a device that can, among other things, ignite a planet [HIGH] S1.

No real proposal is named. "Wormhole" was coined by Wheeler in 1957 and traversable wormholes were not a subject until 1988, twenty years after the film; the Star Gate predates the physics it is now read through. Clarke was well read in general relativity and chose not to invoke it; the passage is written as transport by the unknowable, with the red giant and the white dwarf as the only astrophysics in it. The influence runs forward rather than back: the corridor of light became the visual convention for wormhole transit, reused with acknowledgement in Contact (1997) and Interstellar (2014), whose gates the catalogue treats separately. The naming lineage runs to the Stargate film (1994) and series, which took the word and made the network explicit, and to every "star gate" since. No influence on any physics paper is claimed and none is known.

Which loophole, and why

L3, take a shortcut. The Star Gate is a fixed door: it sits on Japetus, or in Jupiter orbit, it opens when approached, it delivers to a distant place and it belongs to a network of such doors. Nothing moves fast through ordinary space; the pod enters one place and emerges in another. "Grand Central" makes the network reading the novel's own, not a later gloss [HIGH] S1.

The physics cousin is Morris and Thorne 1988, the traversable wormhole, and its bill applies to the gate exactly because the gate is fixed, pre-laid and ancient: negative energy at the throat in quantities scaling with throat radius, mouths delivered at sublight speed in advance (the novel's three million years of waiting is that step, done by someone else) and the time-machine conversion on any relative motion of the mouths. The pinched-off cousin, the Einstein–Rosen bridge, is the reason a slab cannot just be a hole: something must hold it open, and the bridge with nothing holding it closes before light can cross. Clarke's makers, who "learned to store knowledge in the structure of space itself", are the work's answer to what holds it open, and it is not an answer the register can score.

The rules it keeps

  • The gate is where it is. It is found, not made or moved, and its position is signalled from the Moon by a radio burst so a species can be sent to it [HIGH] S1.
  • It is a network. "Grand Central" shows other traffic and other gates, and the novel's makers travel by such gates rather than by ships [HIGH] S1.
  • It carries, it does not respond. Bowman gives no command; the transit is imposed and one-way as depicted [HIGH] S1.
  • It is a tool of the makers, part of a programme that began with the slab in Africa three million years earlier, and it does what the programme requires, including remaking the traveller [HIGH] S1.

The rules are kept from first page to last and through the sequel, and they carry no physics: no energy source, no throat, no travel time given in any frame. That is F2 by the grading scheme, rule-bound with no physical content. The work is not F1, because the gate never does anything the rules did not already allow, and not F3, because it never states a limitation as a limitation; it states silence as a policy.

What it ignores

Time. Bowman goes to another star and comes back, in the novel as the Star-Child in Earth orbit, in the film as the foetus over the Earth, and neither version says how long he was gone by any clock or whether "when" was even the same at both ends [HIGH] S1. The hotel room ages him in what reads as a lifetime; the return is not dated. No character raises relativity at any point in either work. The mark is C-silent, and the silence is characteristic: the same book that gets orbital mechanics, centrifuges and the light delay to Saturn right treats the gate as outside the physics it has been so careful about [HIGH] S1.

The register's other questions, what holds the gate open, what it costs, how a network of gates avoids being a network of time machines, are unasked by design. Absence here is a finding about the work's method: Clarke wrote the gate as the point where explanation stops, and said so in the makers' history.
