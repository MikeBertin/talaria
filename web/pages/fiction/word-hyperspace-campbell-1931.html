Campbell's Islands of Space (1931): where the word came from

The first use of "hyperspace" for a way to beat light, and the thing it named was not a separate space but a bubble of altered space around the ship.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L4 · Notability: N3 · Coherence: F3 · Causality: C-dodges · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

Islands of Space is the second of John W. Campbell's Arcot, Wade and Morey novels, published in the Spring 1931 Amazing Stories Quarterly and revised for the Fantasy Press book of 1957 [HIGH] S3 (Wikipedia, "Islands of Space", publication history). The text read for this dossier is the Project Gutenberg copy, which is the revised book text, so what follows describes the drive as Campbell left it in 1957; the one sentence certainly from 1931 is the dictionary citation, "in the hyperspace we are going into, a new condition exists" [HIGH] S2 (Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction, entry "hyperspace", citing Campbell 1931).

The drive is defined in two conversations. In chapter 1 Arcot explains that the velocity of light is a property of space and that light slows in water because the fields between atoms increase the curvature of the space there; reverse that, "by changing the curvature of the space surrounding you", and any velocity is available, instantaneously and without felt acceleration, because every particle of the ship is moved alike [HIGH] S1 (revised text, chapter 1). In chapter 5 the machinery is shown: a large storage coil that, instead of holding its field inside, affects all the space around it, so that the ship is enclosed in "a hyperspace of our own making" [HIGH] S1 (chapter 5). The word, at its first use for space travel, names a pocket the ship carries, not a place it goes to.

The rules follow in the same conversation and are kept for the rest of the book. The crew cannot see out; they aim the ship, enter the field and come out to look. Stars must be avoided, because a strong gravitational field drains the coils and would drop the ship into the star; meteors do not matter, because the ship is not in normal space to hit them. Gravity also forbids using the drive near the Sun, so the ship runs out past Pluto on its ordinary molecular-motion drive before engaging the "space strain" drive [HIGH] S1 (chapters 5 and 6). In flight the stars appear as violet points ahead and green behind, which Arcot reads as Doppler-shifted fluctuations of their gravitational fields at twenty thousand times the speed of light [HIGH] S1 (chapter 7). Later, having found a planetary system that arrived at its new star before the light of its departure, Arcot lays down the time rule: as far as Earth is concerned the ship moves instantaneously from point to point and returns at the moment it left, while the crew inside the field experience the days of the journey [HIGH] S1 (chapter 9).

Origin and lineage

Campbell wrote the novel at twenty, a year before leaving MIT, for Hugo Gernsback's successors at Amazing Stories Quarterly [MED] S3 (Wikipedia). The word itself is older and mathematical: nineteenth-century textbooks used "hyperspace" for space of more than three dimensions, and Kirk Meadowcroft's "The Invisible Bubble" (1928) used it in fiction three years before Campbell, though not for travel [HIGH] S2 (Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction). The dictionary's citation trail then runs from Campbell through Williamson (1934), Leinster (1934), E. E. Smith (1939), Sturgeon (1941) and Asimov (1951), by which point the word means a separate space that ships pass through rather than a field they carry [HIGH] S2. Gardiner's review in the Journal of the British Interplanetary Society (2008) credits the 1931 text with introducing both hyperspace and the warp drive to science fiction [MED] S2 (as cited by Wikipedia; the paper was not opened). Campbell went on to edit Astounding from 1937, and the pulp default of the computed hyperspace jump grew up in his magazine. The planned lineage page is hyperspace-the-word (lineage).

Which loophole, and why

L4 by catalogue placement, because this is the dossier for the word, and the word came to mean L4. But the drive as depicted is not L4. A field generated by the ship changes the curvature of the space around the ship so that its velocity, as seen from outside, is whatever the field sets; the crew are cut off from the outside while it runs; gravity of nearby masses interferes with it; and the time rule makes the ship's proper time run while the outside sees an instantaneous displacement. That is, in words, an L2 move, and the closest thing in the physics catalogue is Alcubierre 1994: a ship at rest inside a region of altered space which is carried past light, with local light speed never exceeded, tidal force absent at the ship and the pilot causally separated from the wall. Campbell's remarks on light in water are a lay version of Alcubierre's own analogy with inflationary expansion. Alcubierre's bill is what the register says an L2 move costs: negative energy density everywhere in the wall, a total under the quantum inequalities larger than the mass of the visible universe, a wall that cannot be steered from inside and, with two of them, a time machine. Campbell's coil pays none of this, but his description reaches the shape of the thing sixty-three years before the metric.

The L4 reading is covered by Kaluza-Klein and the hyperspace reading and the register's MAN-1: the mathematical hyperspace Campbell borrowed the word from is the same manifold with more directions, not a place with laxer rules. The irony is that Campbell's usage was closer to the physics than what the word became.

The rules it keeps

  • Light speed is a property of space, and the drive changes the space. Stated in chapter 1 and the basis of everything after [HIGH] S1.
  • Gravity interferes. No drive near the Sun; run past Pluto first; stars are the one thing to avoid [HIGH] S1.
  • Blind inside the field, with navigation by aiming before entry and photographing after exit [HIGH] S1.
  • Meteors cannot be hit because the ship is not in normal space [HIGH] S1.
  • Outside time sees an instantaneous displacement; inside time runs. Stated in chapter 9 with a worked example (a week away, gone one day by Earth's reckoning) [HIGH] S1.
  • No radio beyond three hundred light years, because no signals had left Earth before then, and none while in the field [HIGH] S1.

The mark is F3. The novel names the constraint it is evading (light speed as a property of space), states its mechanism in physical terms and applies its own limits consistently for the whole voyage. It does not price the cost, and the physics it offers is wrong on its face (refractive index is not spatial curvature), so F4 is not earned.

What it ignores

The time rule is the interesting omission. Arcot says the ship returns to Earth at the moment it left however long the crew have lived inside the field. That fixes the FTL displacement as instantaneous in Earth's frame, which is a preferred frame in the sense of CAU-4, and the mark is C-dodges on the strength of that stated rule. The novel does not name the dodge: it never asks what an observer moving relative to Earth would see, and the arrival of a planetary system before the light of its departure is treated as a puzzle about light, "rather like traveling in time", and then dropped. Whether the 1931 text had the time rule at all is not verified here; the Gutenberg copy is the 1957 revision [MED] S1. What a curvature-changing coil spends, what sign the energy has and what happens at the boundary of the self-made hyperspace are never addressed, in either version as far as this catalogue can tell, as of 2026-09-12.
