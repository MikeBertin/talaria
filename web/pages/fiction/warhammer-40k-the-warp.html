Warhammer 40,000: the Warp (1987 onward)

A second reality made of emotion and inhabited by things that want in, crossed behind a Gellar field, steered by a mutant who can see it, with time that does not agree with ours.

Kind: fiction · Loophole: L4 · Notability: N2 · Coherence: F3 · Causality: C-owns · Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drive as depicted

The setting began as the tabletop rulebook Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader (Games Workshop, 1987), and has been restated in ten further rulebook editions to 2026, in army codexes, in the Battlefleet Gothic fleet game (1999), in hundreds of Black Library novels and in licensed video games [HIGH] S3 (Wikipedia, "Warhammer 40,000", edition list). The drive is therefore defined by the rulebook background rather than by any one story. The elements are stable across editions. The Warp, also called the Immaterium or the Empyrean, is a parallel dimension of psychic energy, formed and stirred by the emotions of every mind in the material universe and home to the Chaos gods and their daemons [HIGH] S3 (Wikipedia, "Warhammer 40,000", setting). A warp drive tears a hole into it; the ship travels in it, carried by its currents, and drops back into real space having crossed a distance that would take centuries at sublight [MED] S1 (rulebook background, from memory across editions; no rulebook page could be opened this session).

Three rules govern the crossing. A Gellar field, a projected bubble of real-space conditions around the hull, keeps the Warp and its inhabitants out; a failed field means the crew are taken [MED] S3 (from memory; the Lexicanum and fandom articles could not be opened). A Navigator, a member of a mutant human bloodline with a third eye that perceives the Warp, steers by the light of the Astronomican, a psychic beacon projected from Terra by the Emperor's mind; without a Navigator a ship is "limited to warp jumps of only a few light years at a time" [HIGH] S1 (Owlcat Games, Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader, official "How to: Warp Travel" article, 2023, read in full). The 1987 rulebook is reported to give the same short-jump limit for ships without navigational aid, about four light years per jump [LOW] S3 (search summary of a forum thread citing Rogue Trader p. 131; not verified). And time in the Warp does not agree with time outside: a journey of days in the Warp may correspond to weeks or years in real space, the ratio is not reliable and ships have emerged before they departed [MED] S3 (the setting's standard statement, present in the rulebook background of several editions from memory; edition and page not verified this session). Warp storms close routes for years; ships that enter the Warp are sometimes not seen again, or arrive with a dead crew or with something else aboard.

Origin and lineage

Rick Priestley wrote Rogue Trader for Games Workshop in 1987, with the studio's other designers contributing to the background, and the Warp is a deliberate rewrite of the Warhammer fantasy game's Realm of Chaos (1988) as the medium of interstellar travel [MED] S3 (Wikipedia, "Chaos (Warhammer)", which describes the Realm of Chaos as also called the Warp, the Immaterium and the Empyrean). The fictional debts are to Moorcock's Chaos, to Herbert's Navigators (Dune's fold-space, whose Guild navigators use prescience to steer a fold) and to the pulp hyperspace (Campbell 1931 onward), and the setting has never claimed a physics source; the word "warp" is borrowed from Star Trek's vocabulary without its mechanism, and there is no relation to the Alcubierre metric beyond the name [MED] S3. No physics paper cites the setting.

Which loophole, and why

L4, leave the manifold, in its purest form in the catalogue: the Warp is not merely another space but another kind of reality, with inhabitants, weather and its own relation to time, and the ship's whole problem is to stay a bubble of ordinary physics inside it until it can leave. There is no L3 flavour, since there are no fixed corridors; the Astronomican is a lighthouse, not a rail, and stable warp routes are currents that a Navigator can read, not tunnels.

The physics cousin is Kaluza-Klein and the hyperspace reading, the register's MAN-1 worked through: extra dimensions in physics are compact or warped, share our light cone and have no separate time, so a parallel reality with laxer rules and its own clock has no counterpart. The nearest physics proposal to a parallel space with its own time is the fringe Heim theory of Dröscher and Häuser, whose parallel space carries its own light speed and its own clock; that proposal is K1, has no derivations and is charged B-new and B-caus, and the Warp would be charged the same if it were a proposal. The one thing the setting does that physics also does is refuse to give the other space a clock that agrees with ours, and then it draws the consequence.

The rules it keeps

  • The Warp is lethal without a Gellar field, and the field's failure is a recognised catastrophe in every edition [MED] S3.
  • Navigation needs a Navigator and the Astronomican; without them jumps are short and blind (Owlcat 2023; Rogue Trader 1987 as reported) [HIGH] S1.
  • Warp time is not real-space time, in either direction, and the setting uses this constantly: reinforcements arrive decades late, crusades outlive their senders and the Imperium's calendar carries a check digit for how reliable a date is [MED] S3 (the Imperial dating system is a rulebook fixture from memory).
  • Warp storms close routes, and the Astronomican's light has a range, beyond which travel is by short jumps [MED] S3.
  • Entry and exit need a translation point, usually well out from a planet, and a ship can be lost on either [MED] S3.

The mark is F3. The setting states its limitations and applies them consistently across forty years of material, and the limitations are what the fiction is made of. It does not name a real constraint, and the Warp's physics is emotion and daemons rather than any quantity a register could price, so F4 is not earned.

What it ignores

The mark is C-owns, on the time rule. The setting states that the Warp's time does not map onto ours, that the mismatch is unpredictable and that ships have arrived before they left, and it builds the Imperium's whole logistics around not knowing when anything will arrive [MED] S3. It never states the register's form of the problem (CAU-1: FTL in one frame is time travel in another) and never uses frames at all, but it admits the implication that FTL and a consistent timeline cannot both be had, and it uses the admission. That is what C-owns asks for. What it does not do is turn the admission into a paradox: no story that this catalogue knows of has a ship meet itself or a message answered before it was sent, and the missing case is noted as an absence as of 2026-09-12. Energy is never priced at all: the warp drive is a device with a name, and what it spends to tear the hole is not said in any rulebook edition read for this dossier.
