Physicists in the writers' room

The documented cases of a working scientist designing or naming a fictional drive, from Dan Alderson's tramlines in 1974 to Kip Thorne's wormhole in 2014, and which of them produced physics rather than vocabulary.

Kind: lineage · Span: 1974–2017 · Last reviewed: 2026-09-13

The line

This page cuts across the others. Most lineage in the catalogue is a word or a picture travelling on its own; here the carrier is a person with a physics degree who was asked to make a drive for a story. The first well-documented case is Dan Alderson of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, whose friends Larry Niven and Jerry Pournelle handed him a list of what their interstellar drive should allow and forbid and let him design one to the specification. The result is the Alderson drive of The Mote in God's Eye (1974), named for its designer, and the account is the authors' own, in the essay "Building The Mote in God's Eye" (Galaxy, January 1976). The essay has not been read for this catalogue; its story reaches us through Atomic Rockets and the reference wikis, and is graded as such.

The second case is the one with a paper attached. In the summer of 1985 Carl Sagan sent Kip Thorne a draft of Contact and asked for help with the gravity; Thorne and his student Michael Morris found the traversable wormhole solutions, Sagan put them into the novel at galley proof, and the physicists wrote it all up in Morris and Thorne 1988, whose section I.C says so in as many words. That single request produced the Contact machine, a K4 entry on the register and, twenty-six years on, the Interstellar wormhole, for which Thorne served as executive producer and consultant and with the visual effects team published the geometry in the American Journal of Physics in 2015. That line has its own page, sagan-thorne-contact-interstellar. Sean Carroll's part in Thor (2011) is the mirror image: a physicist asked not for physics but for a fresher word. By his own account producer Kevin Feige would not have "wormhole" because it sounded too Nineties, and Carroll offered "Einstein-Rosen bridge" instead, which is how a 1935 paper about a non-traversable throat came to name the Bifrost. The wormhole's own path into film is on morris-thorne-gate-fiction.

Television has kept consultants on staff rather than calling them in. André Bormanis, a physics graduate, was science consultant on the seventh season of The Next Generation and on all of Deep Space Nine and Voyager, wrote the Star Trek: Science Logs book in 1998 and became a writer-producer on Enterprise, before rejoining Brannon Braga on The Orville in 2017, where he designed the quantum drive around Alcubierre's paper by name. The Star Trek warp drive he inherited had been codified in 1991 by Rick Sternbach and Michael Okuda, the show's art department, whose Technical Manual calls them the series' technical advisors and gives the warp factor chart its official form; neither is a physicist and the manual's subspace is not physics, but it is the nearest thing Star Trek has to a definition. Charles Pellegrino, a palaeontologist and writer who co-designed the Valkyrie antimatter rocket at Brookhaven, was technical adviser to James Cameron on Titanic and Avatar, and his 2026 convention biography says the Valkyrie design was animated for the Avatar films as the Venture Star. That is the first statement in his own name found by this catalogue, and it is a convention bio, not a paper. The consultants for the outward Alcubierre line are gathered on star-trek-alcubierre-outward.

Which of these produced physics that survives the register, and which produced only vocabulary? Thorne's two films are the only cases where a consultant's work is itself on the register: the Morris and Thorne wormhole is an exact solution, its bill of negative energy at the throat is stated plainly in both films by handing the engineering to somebody older, and the 2015 paper's lensing is what a wormhole would show. Pellegrino's contribution holds up for the opposite reason: the Venture Star is sublight and the Valkyrie is real engineering with a B-none bill, so nothing on the register touches it. Alderson produced a consistent set of rules with an invented fifth force underneath; the rules are what make the book work and the force has no physics. Bormanis carried a picture and one true line item, the exotic matter, with the production's own accounts disagreeing on whether the drive is a bubble or a higher dimension. Sternbach and Okuda's contribution is the warp factor chart; Carroll's is a name from a 1935 paper whose bridge, on that paper's own terms, cannot be crossed: the Einstein-Rosen bridge pinches shut first.

What is not sourced

  • Alderson's design itself. The essay that tells the story has not been opened, and no page of it is quoted anywhere in the catalogue. The Baen digital edition of the novel reprints it; reading it would let the row above rise to S1 and would settle what Alderson specified and what the authors added.
  • Thorne on Contact in his own voice. The 1988 paper is enough, but the fuller account of the black hole he rejected and the exotic matter he offered is in Black Holes and Time Warps (1994), which the Contact dossier cites without having read.
  • Carroll and the Exchange. Carroll's own post never says how Marvel found him. The Exchange's involvement is inferred from the AAAS profile and from the fact that his wife ran it; a line from the Exchange's own case list would settle it.
  • Pellegrino and the Venture Star, from the production side. The Valkyrie descent now has a statement in Pellegrino's name, but only in a convention biography written for 2026. Nothing from Cameron, Lightstorm or the film's art department has been found, and the Avatar dossier's own finding, that the claim originated on Atomic Rockets and was copied into the press, still stands for everything before 2026.
  • Sternbach and Okuda's warp physics. The manual is a design document, not a physics one, and the catalogue has not re-read chapter 5. Whether its "continuum distortion propulsion" was written with any real paper in view is unknown; Alcubierre's letter came three years later.
  • Bormanis's Star Trek physics. His consulting on the four Trek series is documented as a credit, not as content. Which warp or subspace lines he wrote or corrected is not on record anywhere this catalogue has looked; Science Logs (1998) would be the place to start.
