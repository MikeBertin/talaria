The paper fold

Mark two points on a sheet, fold it so they touch, push a pen through: the one demonstration of a shortcut that every audience has seen, traced from a folded scarf in 1953 to a film that names its own sources.

Kind: lineage · Span: 1953–2022 · Last reviewed: 2026-09-13

The line

The demonstration is older than the wormhole it now explains. In Heinlein's Starman Jones (1953) Max folds a scarf printed with the solar system so that Mars lies on top of Jupiter and asks what stops you "just stepping across"; space, he says, is "crumpled like a piece of waste paper", and an anomaly is a place where it is folded back on itself. Nine years later Madeleine L'Engle gave the trick to Mrs Whatsit in chapter 5 of A Wrinkle in Time (1962), "The Tesseract": Mrs Who holds out the hem of her skirt, an insect would have "quite a long walk" from one hand to the other, and bringing the hands together makes the walk nothing. L'Engle's own sources, by her account, were Einstein and Planck, not Heinlein, and no earlier documented use of a folded sheet as a shortcut was found for this page: not in Gamow's Mr Tompkins, whose folded space is a dream landscape and not a demonstration, and not in the popular relativity books of the 1930s to 1950s consulted at second hand. The scarf of 1953 stands as the earliest sourced instance until someone reads the pulps.

The cinema version has a script. Philip Eisner's shooting draft of Event Horizon (1997) has Dr Weir rip a pin-up from a crewman's locker, mark two points, ask for the shortest distance between them, reject "a straight line", fold the paper so the points meet and thrust his pen through both: the shortest distance "is zero", and that, he says, is what the singularity does. Seventeen years later Romilly does the same thing with a plain sheet in Interstellar (2014), with one improvement: the fold is a picture in two dimensions, so the hole is a circle, and a circle in three dimensions is a sphere, which is what the crew then flies into. Whether the Nolans took the scene from Eisner is not documented; the resemblance was noticed by audiences and reported by the press, and no one on the production has said so. Thor: Love and Thunder (2022) closes the loop by naming its sources on screen. Jane Foster, in a hospital chair, tears a page from her own book, folds it and asks the man next to her whether he has seen Event Horizon or Interstellar; they explain it, she says, really clearly.

On the physics side the fold is a drawing of an embedding diagram, and the diagram is older than any of this. Flamm noticed in 1916 that the space around a Schwarzschild mass embeds as a flared surface, the Einstein–Rosen bridge of 1935 is that surface read as two sheets joined at a throat, and Morris and Thorne 1988 built their teaching paper around the same picture, with the flaring-out condition at the throat stated as a property of the embedded surface. The folded sheet is that surface with the two sheets bent round until they touch, and the pen is the throat. What the fold adds that the physics does not need is the bending itself: an embedding diagram lives in a higher space only for the sake of the drawing, and the fold invites the audience to think the higher space is where the shortcut goes. Every film on this page takes the invitation, which is why Event Horizon and Interstellar are filed as L3 shortcuts with an L4 flavour and not the other way round. The wormholes themselves descend by sagan-thorne-contact-interstellar; the word's spread into television is morris-thorne-gate-fiction.

What is not sourced

  • Anything before 1953. The brief asked whether the fold has a documented use earlier than L'Engle. It does: Heinlein, 1953. Nothing earlier was found, and the search was second-hand. Flammarion was not checked, the 1930s pulps that first "bent" space (Williamson's The Cometeers, 1936, per SFE) were not read, and Gamow's Mr Tompkins (1939 to 1940) describes a fold of space in a dream but, as far as the summaries show, never folds a sheet. A copy of any of those with a folded cloth or paper in it would move the span.
  • Heinlein to L'Engle. No evidence L'Engle read Starman Jones. Her tesseract is a fifth dimension and her word is "wrinkle"; his is an anomaly and his word is "fold". The picture is the same and the descent is unproven.
  • L'Engle or Heinlein to Eisner. No interview, commentary or script note found. Eisner's version is the one every later film copies, and where he got it is the open question.
  • Eisner to the Nolans. The most-repeated claim on this page and the least documented. Every press account traces to audience observation. A line in The Science of Interstellar, a Jonathan Nolan interview or a commentary track would settle it either way.
  • Thor: Love and Thunder's writers. The film names both earlier films in dialogue, which is as explicit as a borrowing gets, but no statement by Taika Waititi or Jennifer Kaytin Robinson about the scene was found; the on-screen citation is the evidence.
  • The physicists' complaint. That the fold misleads, by showing an extrinsic bend where general relativity needs only intrinsic geometry, is a standing objection in teaching forums and has no citable statement of good standing found for this page. It is recorded as an absence, dated 2026-09-13.
